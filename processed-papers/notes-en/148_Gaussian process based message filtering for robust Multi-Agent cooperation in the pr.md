# 148. Gaussian Process Based Message Filtering for Robust Multi-Agent Cooperation in the Presence of Adversarial Communication

## Taxonomy
- **Robustness / perturbation type targeted**: Adversarial / non-cooperative communication (faulty, misleading or manipulative messages from anonymous non-cooperative agents); communication attacks / fault tolerance in multi-agent systems.
- **Method paradigm**: Probabilistic message filtering via confidence weighting; Gaussian Process (GP) prior over per-agent autoencoder latents (AEVB/VAE); Bayesian hypothesis testing (truth vs. plausible/implausible lie); attention-like modification of a GNN aggregation layer.
- **Keywords**: Adversarial communication, Gaussian Processes, Graph Neural Networks, message filtering, confidence weighting, multi-agent robustness

## TL;DR
The paper proposes a GP-based probabilistic model of the position-dependent mutual information between spatially proximate agents' message encodings, and uses it to compute per-neighbor "confidence" (posterior probability of truthfulness) that weights a GNN aggregation layer, thereby suppressing the influence of anonymous non-cooperative / adversarial communications with negligible cost when no adversary is present.

## Problem & Motivation
Decentralized multi-agent control and communication policies (e.g., learned GNN-based communication) are inherently fragile to unexpected behavior of any single agent — including failures, purposeful adversarial attacks, and Byzantine collusion — unless explicitly designed to be robust. Most multi-agent learning approaches assume full cooperation (all agents maximize a shared global reward), and there is a dearth of work on learning communication policies that are robust to non-cooperative or adversarial communications from neighbors. The paper targets robust cooperation when F anonymous, unknown non-cooperative agents communicate faulty, misleading, or manipulative information; the non-cooperative agents' behavior is unavailable to train against (unknown) and cannot be distinguished from cooperative agents except by examining their communication (anonymous). Unlike encoding-based anomaly detection that flags individual anomalous observations, the goal is to detect anomalous observations within sets of spatially inter-related observation encodings.

## Robustness Setting
- **Threat model / uncertainty set**: N agents in 2D, of which N−F are cooperative (homogeneous, sharing enc/agg/pol) and F are anonymous non-cooperative agents that differ at least in their encoding function and may send manipulative messages. The adversary is characterized by a four-level taxonomy of knowledge about the cooperative agents: Faulty (no goal, simply faulty), Naive (knows encC, polC and effect of aggC on normal communication, but unaware of detection mechanisms), Cautious (knows a detection mechanism may exist but not its specifics, so limits manipulation to the range of normal cooperative communication), and Omniscient (perfect knowledge of communication and detection strategies, optimizes directly against them). Cooperative agents have no knowledge of the adversary's goal or strategy, and the cooperative communication strategy is designed without knowing the adversary's level of knowledge.
- **Setting**: Cooperative (with self-interested / non-cooperative agent in the RL experiment); fully decentralized, localized one-hop GNN communication (decentralizable policies); online sequential RL in the coverage experiment, plus a static one-shot perception (image classification) experiment. Only one round of messages per time instant; only intra-time-instant information used (no inter-temporal consistency check).

## Method
- **Auto-encoding communication model**: Each agent encodes local observations o_i into a message m_i = q(z|o)_i (a diagonally covariant Gaussian latent posterior) using a per-agent VAE encoder (AEVB framework). To capture that nearby agents observing the same world share mutual information, the per-agent independent latent priors are replaced by a joint Gaussian Process prior over agent positions, P(Z|X; ψ) = GP(0, K_ψ(X)), with a learnable kernel function; the decoder factorizes per agent.
- **Hypotheses for truthfulness**: For each neighbor, three single-agent hypotheses are compared — H0 (Truth: observations generated by the best-fit world model and correlated as expected by physical position), H1 (Plausible Lie: latent drawn independently from the GP, uncorrelated with others), and H2 (Implausible Lie: latent drawn from a high-variance / uniform distribution in latent space).
- **Confidence weights**: Neighborhood hypotheses (assignments of H0/H1/H2 to each agent) are scored by an approximate log-likelihood (−KL between the variational posterior and the hypothesis-specific prior, plus a constant) combined with hypothesis priors via Bayes' theorem; marginalizing yields c_j(i), the posterior probability that agent i is truthful from agent j's perspective. Two sensitivity parameters s1, s2 (log-prior differences) remain tunable; the set of hypotheses can encode a known maximum number of adversaries F (or F_max).
- **Message filtering layer**: The confidence c_i(j) is inserted as an attention-like weight in a sum-aggregation GNN layer (Eq. 12). The modified layer reduces to the standard unmodified GNN layer when all confidences approach 1 (no adversaries / full trust), and completely negates a neighbor's message contribution in the low-confidence limit.
- **Training**: Decoder (θ), encoder (φ), and GP kernel (ψ) are fit by maximizing an approximate evidence lower bound: a pairwise KL loss for the kernel, plus a β-weighted KL loss and reconstruction loss for the encoder/decoder (with a fallback when covariance determinants are negative early in training). The kernel depends only on relative position; GP cost is bounded because only the agent's neighborhood is considered simultaneously.

## Theoretical Contributions
None / mostly empirical. The contributions are methodological/probabilistic derivations (the GP+VAE communication model, the hypothesis-comparison and confidence-weight derivation in Appendix I, and the relative-position kernel construction in Appendix II) rather than formal convergence, sample-complexity, or certified-robustness guarantees. The paper notes that no message-filtering strategy with negligible impact on purely cooperative agents can reliably detect/filter all possible adversaries, since a sufficiently capable adversary can produce communication arbitrarily similar to cooperative communication.

## Experiments
- **Environment/Benchmark**: (1) Cooperative image classification: CIFAR-10 restricted to two classes (airplanes/automobiles), 10,000 training and 2000 test images; N = 6 agents each observing a 9×9 local grid, fully connected communication; estimate the global categorical state. (2) Cooperative coverage (multi-agent coverage path planning in an RL setting, following [6]): non-convex 24×24 binary grid world, N = 6 agents with a 16×16 local field of view and communication radius r_c = 16; one self-interested non-cooperative agent (F = 1) competes for coverage.
- **Baselines**: Image classification: No weighting; WMAX (threshold on squared message magnitude); WG (per-message individual plausibility, ignoring inter-agent correlations); compared against the proposed WGP scheme. Coverage: Random baseline; Cooperative trained without VAE/GP; Cooperative with pre-trained VAE (with/without confidence weighting); drop-in median filter (benchmark alternative); without communication.
- **Evaluation metrics**: Image classification: cooperative loss distribution and mean, classification accuracy, and distribution of confidence weights assigned to adversaries; evaluated across adversary knowledge classes (Faulty/Naive/Cautious/Omniscient) with F ∈ {0,1} and over varying F and F_max with N = 8. Coverage: percentual global coverage at fixed time steps t0 = 58 and t1 = 345 (p0, p1) for cooperative agents, non-cooperative agent, and all agents.

## Key Results
- Image classification: In the absence of adversaries, WGP causes only a negligible ~0.1% increase in shared loss. Without weighting, Faulty/Naive/Cautious/Omniscient adversaries increase loss by 4.7%, 578%, 61.8%, and 34.2% respectively; introducing WGP reduces these increases by 33%, 99.7%, 97.5%, and 15%, almost entirely negating the Naive and Cautious adversaries. WGP assigns consistently low confidence to Faulty/Naive/Cautious adversaries (and detects/filters Faulty and Cautious perfectly), but cannot easily detect the Omniscient adversary.
- WGP is superior to WMAX and WG over a wider range of adversaries; e.g., against respective Omniscient adversaries WGP incurs excess loss 0.148 vs. WG's 0.312 (a factor of two in WGP's favor), and the scheme generalizes across varying F and F_max as long as F_max ≥ F.
- Coverage (RL): For the Naive case without confidence weighting the non-cooperative agent covers 430% (p1) of an average cooperative agent's area and team coverage drops to 64% (p0) of an all-cooperative team; with confidence weighting the non-cooperative agent covers only 82% and total performance rises to 92%. In the Omniscient case, confidence weighting yields similar (slightly better) performance even though the adversary was trained with knowledge of the weighting. The drop-in median filter substantially harms cooperative performance even without adversaries (~71% of the no-VAE cooperative baseline), whereas confidence weighting has no negative impact when no adversary is present.

## Limitations & Future Work
- No filtering strategy with negligible cost on cooperative agents can reliably detect/filter an Omniscient adversary, since a capable adversary can mimic cooperative communication arbitrarily closely; WGP only bounds the damage of the strongest attack that can bypass it.
- The chosen stationary GP kernel (relative-position-only) causes occasional invalid covariance matrices early in training; this could be avoided if stationarity were not required (e.g., access to absolute position) or by approximately enforcing stationarity (e.g., randomly translating training data). Validity for N ≥ 3 is not guaranteed but is observed empirically after training on pairs.
- The method deliberately ignores several information sources (non-communication behavior, inter-temporal consistency, only crude precision checks). Future work: use all previously observed communication for confidence weighting, and check consistency between agents' behavior and their claimed observations given the known cooperative policy — both would further reduce an Omniscient adversary's available strategies.

## Relevance to Survey
This paper sits on the "communication robustness / adversarial communication" line of robust MARL, addressing learned (GNN-based) communication under anonymous non-cooperative agents rather than environment/model uncertainty or state/action perturbation. It connects the robust-consensus / resilient-flocking literature (and (r,s)-robustness) to learning-based multi-agent communication, and complements detection approaches (watermarking, physical fingerprinting) by exploiting semantic inconsistency between spatially correlated message encodings. It is closely related to work on emergent adversarial communication in MARL (its own [6]) and to attention-based GNN communication, and provides a probabilistic message-filtering paradigm relevant to the survey's communication-attack / fault-tolerance themes.

## Related Work (verbatim excerpts from the paper)
> _[Introduction]_

"Many real-world problems require the coordination of multiple autonomous agents [1], [2]. In fully decentralized systems, agents not only need to know how to cooperate, but also, how to communicate to most effectively coordinate their actions in pursuit of a common goal. However, control policies and communication protocols designed to optimally coordinate the behavior of multiple agents are inherently fragile with respect to unexpected behavior from any one of these agents, unless they are specifically designed to be robust. This fragility has been observed in the case of failures or malfunctions [3], [4], in the case of purposeful (adversarial) attacks [5], [6], as well as in worst-case scenarios, such as byzantine collusion [7], [8]."

> _[Introduction]_

"Recent work has shown the promise of GNNs to learn explicit communication strategies that enable complex multi-agent coordination [9], [10], [11], [12]. GNNs exploit the fact that inter-agent relationships can be represented as graphs, which provide a mathematical description of the network topology. In multi-agent systems, an agent is modeled as a node in the graph, the connectivity of agents as edges, and the internal state of an agent as a graph signal. The key attribute of GNNs is that they operate in a localized manner, whereby information is shared over a multi-hop communication network through explicit communication with nearby neighbors only, hence resulting in fully decentralizable policies."

> _[Introduction]_

"While impressive results have been achieved, these multi-agent learning approaches assume full cooperation, whereby all agents share the same goal of maximizing a shared global reward. There is a dearth of work that explores whether agents can utilize machine learning to synthesize communication policies that are robust to non-cooperative or even adversarial communications from their neighbors."

> _[Introduction]_

"While there is a rich body of work on encoding-based anomaly detection (e.g., [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]), the aforementioned setting distinguishes our work. Instead of detecting individual anomalous observations, we are interested detecting anomalous observations within sets of observation encodings that are spatially inter-related. Specifically, we expect the encodings communicated by spatially proximate agents to share substantial mutual information, and that the structure of this non-independence will itself be dependent on the spatial arrangement of these agents in the world they are observing."

> _[Introduction — "Related Work." paragraph]_

"There is a recent body of work addressing multi-agent robustness from the perspective of robust consensus in the presence of non-cooperative agents [4], [20], [21]. These methods provide guarantees of robustness up to some maximum number of non-cooperative agents subject to the condition of sufficient redundant connectivity (defined by (r, s)-robustness metrics [22], [4], [23]) in the agents' communication network. The key difference between these methods and ours is that they aim to robustly reach a consensus on specific global values based on local information. We generalize this approach, as the class of robust communication problems we consider do not necessarily involve the explicit estimation of any such global values. Outlier-robust estimation algorithms such as RANSAC [24], [25], [26] are inapplicable to our class of problems for similar reasons."

> _[Introduction — "Related Work." paragraph]_

"We identify two broad classes of previous work applicable to our class of problems. Some authors focus on optimizing the collective control policy itself for robustness to communication or hardware failure [27], [28], while others focus on the detection of malicious communication, either by dynamic watermarking, physical fingerprinting or inconsistencies between reported location and directional variations in wireless signal strength [5], [29], [30]. None of these methods exploit the same source of information as ours: inconsistencies between the semantic implications of received messages, and so while they may be complementary to our approach they are fundamentally different in character."

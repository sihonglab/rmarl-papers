# 34. RoMFAC: A robust mean-field actor-critic reinforcement learning against adversarial perturbations on states

## Metadata

- **Authors**: Ziyuan Zhou, Guanjun Liu
- **Affiliation**: Department of Computer Sciences, Tongji University, Shanghai 201804, China

## Taxonomy
- **Robustness / perturbation type targeted**: State / observation perturbation (adversarial perturbations on the states observed by agents; multiple agents may be attacked, with the number of perturbed agents unknown and inter-agent influence)
- **Method paradigm**: Adversarial training with action-loss regularization, mean-field actor-critic, minimax (worst-case state) optimization, PGD adversary, game-theoretic formulation (state-adversarial stochastic game), policy regularization extending SA-MDP to MARL
- **Keywords**: Mean-Field Actor-Critic, state perturbation, adversarial robustness, action loss, repetitive regularization, State-Adversarial Stochastic Game (SASG)

## TL;DR
The paper proposes RoMFAC, a robust mean-field actor-critic method that defends against adversarial state perturbations by adding an action-loss term (difference between actions on clean vs. adversarial states) with a novel repetitive regularization, and formalizes a State-Adversarial Stochastic Game (SASG) in which adversarial perturbations are provably defensible even though a Nash equilibrium may not exist.

## Problem & Motivation
Multi-agent deep RL bases decisions on observed system states, but observations can be perturbed by sensor errors or malicious attacks, misleading agents into wrong actions (critical for autonomous driving, UAVs, robotics). Mean-Field Actor-Critic (MFAC) solves the scalability problem of MARL with many agents but is found to be sensitive to state perturbations, which significantly degrade team rewards and reduce safety. While adversarial attacks/defenses are studied for single-agent RL, there are few studies in the multi-agent field, where additional challenges arise: (1) the total number of perturbed agents is unknown, and (2) perturbations on some agents can influence others. The work aims to make MFAC robust to state perturbations while preserving clean-state performance.

## Robustness Setting
- **Threat model / uncertainty set**: Adversarial perturbations are applied to the states observed by agents. Each agent j's adversarial state is ŝʲ = s + δʲ, constrained to a set Bʲ of adversarial states; perturbations are generated by a white-box PGD attack with ℓ∞ norm bound ε. In the SASG formulation, the adversarial perturbation vʲ(s) is a deterministic, time-invariant function vʲ: S → Bʲ that perturbs only agent j's state while the environment itself stays unchanged; M ≤ N agents may be attacked, and the joint optimal adversary minimizes each attacked agent's expected cumulative discounted reward.
- **Setting**: Cooperative and competitive / mixed (Battle is cooperative-competitive, Pursuit is local cooperation); actor-critic with mean-field approximation; online, on-policy training; robustness is enforced on the actors (critics only guide actor updates).

## Method
- **Action loss function**: To learn a robust policy, RoMFAC maximizes the worst-case expected cumulative discounted reward, i.e., max over actor parameters of min over adversarial state ŝʲ (Eq. 9). The inner minimization is solved by maximizing the "action loss" — the cross-entropy difference between the action distribution on clean states and on adversarial states, where the target label zʲ is the highest-probability action under the clean state (Eq. 10).
- **PGD adversary**: The adversarial perturbation δʲ for the policy network is generated by multi-step projected gradient descent (PGD) gradient ascent on the action loss, with step-size β and clipping (Eq. 11).
- **Combined actor objective**: The actor πθʲ is trained by minimizing −∇θʲ J(θʲ) + μ·Lact(θʲ) (Eq. 12), where the first term is the standard policy gradient on clean states and μ trades off robustness against clean performance.
- **Repetitive regularization of the action loss**: A fixed μ is problematic (too large → vanishing/exploding gradients and unstable training; too small → action loss ineffective). RoMFAC instead trains in two steps: Step 1 trains the network to stability with μ = 0 (Mnorm rounds); Step 2 continues for c loops, in each of which μ increases linearly from 0 up to an upper bound, while the perturbation bound ε stays fixed (Eq. 13). This is unlike standard single-agent robust training that uses grid-searched fixed μ and a gradually increasing ε.
- The full procedure is given in Algorithm 1 (sample actions, compute mean action ā, store transitions in replay buffer, update critic via Eq. 7, generate adversarial states via PGD, compute action loss, update actor via Eqs. 12–13, and soft-update target networks).

## Theoretical Contributions
- Defines the State-Adversarial Stochastic Game (SASG) (Definition 1) extending the stochastic game with adversarial-state sets B¹,…,Bᴹ.
- Theorem 1: Bellman equations for fixed joint policy π and joint adversary v.
- Theorem 2: For the joint optimal adversarial perturbation, the per-agent Bellman operator Lʲ is a contraction converging to a unique fixed point; hence the joint optimal adversarial perturbation v*(s) is unique and the proposed action loss (Eq. 10) is convergent.
- Theorem 3: Under the joint optimal adversarial perturbation v*, the Nash equilibrium of SASG may not always exist.
- Theorem 4: Bounds the value-function degradation under adversarial states by the total-variation distance between action distributions on clean vs. adversarial states (Eq. 14), with constant ζ = 2(1 + γ/(1−γ)²)·maxₛ,ₐ,ₛ′ Rʲ(s,a,s′); thus robust policies can be trained even without a Nash equilibrium, and the method can extend to other MARL approaches. (Full proofs in Appendices A–D of the supplementary file.)

## Experiments
- **Environment/Benchmark**: Two scenarios of MAgent [Zheng et al., 2018], which can support hundreds of agents. Battle: cooperative-competitive, two groups (A and B) of 64 agents each (128 total) trying to eliminate the opponent. Pursuit: local cooperation, 32 predators and 64 prey.
- **Baselines**: MFAC (no robust strategy); SA-MFAC (fixed weight factor μ and increased perturbation bound ε, like most robust training); SA-MFAC3 (SA-MFAC with the proposed repetitive regularization, i.e., constant μ but repetitively changing ε); RoMFAC1 (ablation: μ uses a single linear increase, one loop). Testing opponents/prey use advantageous actor critic (A2C) policies and their states are not perturbed.
- **Evaluation metrics**: For Battle: winning rate, average kills, average total reward. For Pursuit: average total reward. Robustness is evaluated under a 10-step PGD ℓ∞ attack with perturbation budget ε = 0.075, over 50 testing rounds with max 400 time steps, varying the number of attacked agents (0, 8, 16, 32, 48, 64).

## Key Results
- RoMFAC consistently achieves the best robustness: e.g., in Battle its winning rate stays ≥ 0.86 across all attacked-agent counts (1.00 at 0 attacked, 0.90 at 64 attacked), whereas MFAC's winning rate collapses to 0.00 once 32 or more agents are attacked.
- Robust training also improves clean-state (0 attacked) performance: RoMFAC reaches winning rate 1.00, average kill 63.98, average total reward 320.29 in Battle and 3844.66 in Pursuit, outperforming all baselines on clean states.
- Comparing RoMFAC1 vs. RoMFAC shows the repetitive regularization is significant; applying repetitive regularization to SA-MFAC (SA-MFAC3) also improves results, especially in the Pursuit scenario.
- Qualitatively (Figure 3), unprotected MFAC agents lose learned cooperation under perturbation (attacking empty grids in Battle; scattered, ineffective siege in Pursuit).

## Limitations & Future Work
- Only the robustness of the actors is considered (critics are treated only as guides for actor updates), and the analysis assumes a deterministic, time-invariant adversary vʲ(s).
- The SASG may have no Nash equilibrium under the joint optimal adversarial perturbation (Theorem 3); robustness is argued via a value-degradation bound rather than equilibrium guarantees.
- The authors note that without high-dimensional image input, standard fixed-μ / increasing-ε regularization gives non-ideal results, motivating the repetitive scheme; hyper-parameters c and μ must be tuned.
- Future work: extend the method (action loss + repetitive regularization) to other MARL approaches besides MFAC.

## Relevance to Survey
RoMFAC sits squarely on the "state/observation-perturbation robustness" line of robust MARL, and is the multi-agent extension of the single-agent SA-MDP / policy-regularization framework of Zhang et al. (2020). It connects the robust single-agent RL literature (SA-MDP, RADIAL-RL, learned-adversary alternating training) to scalable mean-field MARL, and contributes a game-theoretic formalization (SASG) for adversarial state attacks/defenses in MARL. It complements opponent-policy-robustness work such as M3DDPG (which it explicitly distinguishes from, since M3DDPG lacks defense against state perturbations) and adversarial-example-generation work in MARL (Lin et al., 2020), positioning it within the "adversarial training + game-theoretic robustness" method line.

## Related Work (verbatim excerpts from the paper)
> _[Section 2, Related Works — "Adversarial Attacks on Single-agent DRL"]_

"In classification tasks, the methods for generating and defending against adversarial examples have been extensively studied. Adversarial attacks and defenses for deep reinforcement learning have recently emerged. The adversarial attacks on DRL algorithms can be broadly divided into four categorizes [Ilahi et al., 2021]: the state space with adversarial perturbations, the reward function with adversarial perturbations, the action space with adversarial perturbations and the model space with adversarial perturbations. Huang et al. [2017] employ FGSM [Goodfellow et al., 2014] to generate adversarial examples of input states, showing that adversarial attacks are also effective in the DRL policy network. To make the attack on DRL agents more stealthy and efficient, Sun et al. [2020] introduce two adversarial attack techniques: the critical point attack and the antagonist attack. This paper is about state perturbations."

> _[Section 2, Related Works — "Robust Training for Single-agent DRL"]_

"Defense methods against attacks are broadly classified into six categories [Ilahi et al., 2021]: adversarial training, defensive distillation, robust learning, adversarial detection, benchmarking & watermarking and game theoretic approach. Zhang et al. [2020] propose state-adversarial Markov decision process (SA-MDP), which provides a theoretical foundation for robust single-agent reinforcement learning. They develop the principle of policy regularization that can possibly be applied to many DRL algorithms. Based on SA-MDP, an alternate training framework with learned adversaries was proposed [Zhang et al., 2021]. Oikarinen et al. [2020] propose the RADIAL-RL method, which can improve the robustness of DRL agents under the ℓp norm boundary against attacks, with lower computational complexity. This paper focuses on robust learning and expands the theoretical results and policy regularization in SA-MDP to multi-agent DRL."

> _[Section 2, Related Works — "Adversarial Attacks and Defenses for Multi-agent DRL"]_

"Motivated by single-agent deep reinforcement learning [Mnih et al., 2015], multi-agent reinforcement learning has changed from the tabular method to the deep learning recently. However, there are few studies on adversarial attacks and robust training in multi-agent DRL. Lin et al. [2020] first propose the method of adversarial examples generation in MARL, but do not provide a robust defense method. Li et al. [2019] propose the M3DDPG, which is an extension of MADDPG that makes policies of agents generalizing even if the opponent's policies change. They also present a robust optimization method, which effectively solves the problem of high complexity of minmax calculation in continuous action space. However, they lack the defense against state perturbations that is exactly the purpose of our work."

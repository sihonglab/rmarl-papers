# 110. Robust Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning Against Adversarial Attacks for Cooperative Self-Driving Vehicles

## Metadata
- **Title**: Robust Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning Against Adversarial Attacks for Cooperative Self-Driving Vehicles
- **Authors**: Chuyao Wang, Ziwei Wang, Nabil Aouf
- **Affiliation**: Department of Engineering, School of Science and Technology, City St George's, University of London, London, UK
- **Venue**: IET Radar, Sonar & Navigation 2025 (Vol. 19, e70033)
- **Link/arXiv**: https://doi.org/10.1049/rsn2.70033

## Taxonomy
- **Robustness / perturbation type targeted**: State/observation perturbation (white-box adversarial attacks on each agent's camera observations and on shared/communicated observations); safety constraints; sensor errors / environment uncertainty in cooperative self-driving.
- **Method paradigm**: Robust constrained MARL; adversarial training with worst-case (max-min) objective; mean-field communicated multi-agent actor-critic; risk estimation network; constrained optimisation via Lagrangian duality; control barrier function (CBF); KL/total-variation divergence regulariser; multi-agent PPO.
- **Keywords**: robust MARL, adversarial attacks, cooperative self-driving, mean-field theory, risk assessment, control barrier function, FGSM/BIM

## TL;DR
The paper proposes R-CCMARL, a robust constrained cooperative MARL algorithm for self-driving vehicles that combines a universal (nontask-oriented) policy with mean-field communication, a collision-risk estimation network, and a constrained max-min objective (with a divergence regulariser and a control-barrier-function safety constraint) to keep cooperative intersection-passing policies robust against strong white-box observation perturbations on one or all agents.

## Problem & Motivation
MARL improves coordination, efficiency, and stability for cooperative self-driving compared with single-agent DRL, but it remains highly vulnerable to adversarial attacks: perturbed observation states can lead one or more vehicles into critical decision errors, triggering chain reactions and severe collisions. Robustness is needed not only against deliberate cyberattacks but also against unavoidable sensor errors and equipment inaccuracies. Most prior MARL works train agents only on local observations and design task-specific policies (e.g., predator vs. prey, fixed football roles), which is ill-suited for autonomous driving where every vehicle must handle a wide range of manoeuvres. Robust single-agent defences (adversarial-sample data augmentation, certified robustness, SA-MDP regularisation) bring limited improvement and overlook MARL-specific challenges: attacked agents' subsequent behaviour affects other agents, and shared/communicated information can itself be perturbed, propagating errors across the system.

## Robustness Setting
- **Threat model / uncertainty set**: White-box, gradient-based adversarial attacks on observations. The attacker generates optimal adversarial states via an augmented FGSM with Basic Iterative Method (BIM) iterations, using an "unintended-action-leading" loss that maximises throttle and reverses steering to deliberately maximise collision risk (rather than simply minimising long-term reward). Attacks are applied to the targeted agent's camera observation; shared information from neighbours can also be perturbed. Bounded worst-case perturbation: the L2 norm of (g(s,π) − s) is bounded by μ. Attack strength ε ∈ [0,1] (experiments use ε = 0.1 with 20 iterations). The number of agents that may be compromised is unknown.
- **Setting**: Cooperative; centralised-training-with-decentralised-execution (CTDE); communicated multi-agent (information sharing under mean-field theory); online / model-free; Stochastic Games (SG) formulation with worst-case perturbed joint state.

## Method
- Formulate cooperative driving as a Stochastic Game augmented with a worst-case perturbed joint state, with the robust objective Q^i_{π*} = max_π min_{S_A} E[Σ γ^t r^i_t(s^i_t / s^i_{A,t}, a^i_t / a^i_{A,t})] (outer max over policy, inner min over adversarial state).
- Use a single universal policy network shared by all agents and a mean-field actor-critic decomposition so each agent reasons over its local observation plus a fused "mean state" of neighbours, processed through two FC layers and attention modules; this enables nontask-oriented driving agents and scalable interaction modelling.
- Solve the inner minimisation with a gradient-based white-box attacker (augmented FGSM + BIM) whose loss L^i_n = −2·(steer^i | s^i_n) + 1 − (throttle^i | s^i_n) encourages dangerous accelerating/steering, producing the optimal adversary s^i_A.
- Define a collision-risk formulation: each vehicle is a circle, pairwise risk P_risk is the time-interval overlap ratio in the collision zone, and total system risk P_tot = 1 − Π(1 − P_risk,i); a risk network S_θ is trained (MSE) to predict long-term safety-to-go, and a risk-value-based credit-assignment term Credit^i augments the advantage to reflect each agent's contribution.
- Solve the outer maximisation with a robust constrained objective: extend multi-agent PPO (clipped surrogate) with (i) long-term risk minimisation, (ii) a divergence-based adversarial regulariser L^i_reg = sqrt((1/2) D_KL(π(s), π(s_A))) (motivated by total-variation/Pinsker bounding the value gap), and (iii) two constraints — a bounded-perturbation constraint and a CBF safety constraint h(s) = S_θ(s) with a Lie-derivative condition. The constrained problem is converted via Lagrangian duality into an unconstrained max-min and solved by alternating gradient descent/ascent on the policy and the multipliers α, β (Algorithm 1, R-CCMARL).

## Theoretical Contributions
- Mostly empirical / algorithmic. The paper provides supporting derivations rather than formal convergence proofs: it extends a value-gap bound (from Ref. [49]) showing max value gap ≤ max total-variation distance D_TV between action distributions under clean and attacked states, and uses Pinsker's inequality to bound D_TV by KL divergence, justifying the KL regulariser. It also gives a Lagrangian-duality argument that the dual problem lower-bounds the primal, and a CBF/Lie-derivative safety condition under a Lipschitz-continuity assumption on well-trained model states.

## Experiments
- **Environment/Benchmark**: CARLA simulator (Unreal-Engine powered), Town 2; intersection-passing tasks with three cooperative vehicles. Scenario 1: T-shaped intersection (Agent 1 goes straight, Agents 2 and 3 turn left), two different intersections for randomness. Scenario 2: 4-way intersection with 7 autopilot surrounding vehicles. Each agent inputs a 160×80 monocular RGB image plus 5 numeric features (throttle, velocity, steer, distance to road centre, angle to waypoint). 10 fps, single RTX 4090.
- **Baselines**: MAPPO (Scenario 1); ERNIE (Scenario 2, adapted by replacing its network with the paper's while keeping its robust training formulation); a "risk-only" variant of the proposed method (no robust constraints). Attack config: ε = 0.1, iteration = 20.
- **Evaluation metrics**: Average Team Reward, Average Individual Reward, Average Individual Success Rate, Average Team Risk, Average Individual Risk; evaluated under four attack strategies (attack each single agent, attack all agents) plus no-attack; 50 episodes per category.

## Key Results
- In Scenario 1, R-CCMARL leads all categories (success rate, return, risk) under every attack strategy. Under "Attack on All", MAPPO collapses (e.g., Agents 2 and 3 reach 0% success), while R-CCMARL retains 0.90 / 0.78 / 0.84 success for Agents 1/2/3. In normal conditions R-CCMARL reaches ~203.04 team reward vs MAPPO's 136.15 (about 49.13% gain) and ~28.5% over the risk-only model.
- Attacks on different agents have different impact; Agent 2 is the most vulnerable. Under per-agent attacks, R-CCMARL improves team reward by 43.4%/16.3% over MAPPO/risk-only (Agent 2 attacked) and 44.8%/20.3% (Agent 3 attacked); under all-agents attack it holds 136.9 vs 91.7 (MAPPO) and 106.2 (risk-only).
- In Scenario 2, R-CCMARL consistently outperforms ERNIE; under "all agents attacked" it sustains 56%/58%/42% success vs ERNIE's 30%/18%/24%, and team reward 126.99 vs ERNIE's 55.93 (about 127% increase). R-CCMARL also shows lower team risk overall and smaller action (steering/throttle) deviations between attacked and normal conditions, indicating stronger resilience.

## Limitations & Future Work
- Constraint thresholds μ and e are selected manually through experimentation and are sensitive to the scenario, significantly affecting training stability and performance.
- The method is model-free and relies on a Lipschitz-continuity assumption to approximate system dynamics via state differences for the CBF constraint.
- Evaluation is limited to the CARLA simulator (intersection scenarios, three agents); no real-vehicle / sim-to-real results are reported. Explicit future-work directions are Not specified.

## Relevance to Survey
This paper sits on the "state/observation-perturbation robustness" line of robust MARL, specialised to safety-critical cooperative autonomous driving. It connects the adversarial-attack-and-defence theme (FGSM/BIM white-box attacks; SA-MDP, RADIAL-RL, policy smoothing, robust mean-field actor-critic, ERNIE-style adversarial regularisation) with the communication-robustness theme (perturbed shared observations) and the safety-constraint theme (risk estimation + control barrier functions). It complements game-theoretic robust-Markov-game works by emphasising a practical, scalable, constrained-optimisation defence with mean-field communication, and is a useful applied data point on robustness of cooperative MARL under coordinated observation attacks.

## Related Work (verbatim excerpts from the paper)

### MARL for Autonomous Driving
> _[Section 2.1, Related Work — MARL for Autonomous Driving]_

"Multi‐agent deep reinforcement learning (MARL) aims to maximise team rewards, and several effective approaches have been developed. MADDPG [7] extends DDPG to multi‐agent settings, where each agent has its own actor and critic networks, using global information to learn coordinated policies. MAAC (Multi‐Agent Actor‐Critic) [20] improves coordination by using attention mechanisms to prioritise relevant information from other agents, making it more efficient in complex environments. G2ANet [21] further enhances communication by incorporating graph attention networks to capture agent interactions dynamically. On the value‐based side, QMIX uses a mixing network to combine individual Q‐values into a global Q‐value, ensuring a more accurate global Q‐value estimation. QPD [22] decomposes the global Q‐function into individual components for better scalability, while QPLEX [23] uses duplex duelling networks to model the interplay between agents. Mean‐filed actor‐critic method (MFAC) [24] applies the mean‐field theory to MARL and thus successfully improves the scalability of MARL with a large number of agents."

> _[Section 2.1, Related Work — MARL for Autonomous Driving]_

"Following the major breakthroughs of MARL in recent years, recent advancements in multi‐agent deep reinforcement learning (MARL) have expanded the scope of autonomous driving systems by addressing complex interactions among multiple vehicles and agents. Unlike single‐agent DRL, which focuses on optimising the performance of an individual vehicle, MARL considers the collaborative and competitive dynamics in a shared driving environment [25, 26]. This approach is particularly useful for scenarios involving multiple autonomous vehicles that must navigate and negotiate their movements in real‐time. MARL techniques can significantly enhance the coordination and cooperation among vehicles, improving overall traffic flow, safety, and efficiency. These methods allow vehicles to learn not only from their own experiences but also from the interactions with other agents, leading to more robust decision‐making and adaptive behaviours [2, 27, 28]. For instance, D. Li et al. [29] introduce a cooperative control framework where autonomous vehicles use MARL to synchronise their movements and optimise lane merging and intersection crossing, resulting in smoother traffic management and reduced congestion. Furthermore, MARL can address the challenge of nonstationary environments where the behaviour of other agents is dynamic and uncertain. By leveraging multi‐agent techniques, vehicles can develop strategies to anticipate and respond to the actions of neighbouring vehicles more effectively [30]. H. Lin et al. [31] propose an enhanced state representation for MARL‐based platoon‐following models by integrating inter‐vehicle dynamics and global traffic information, achieving improved stability over standard MARL baselines. Z. Huang et al. [32] present a method that integrates MARL with communication protocols, allowing vehicles to share information about their intentions and local environment, thereby improving coordination and reducing the likelihood of accidents."

> _[Section 2.1, Related Work — MARL for Autonomous Driving]_

"However, MARL systems face notable challenges, particularly regarding their vulnerability to adversarial attacks. The collaborative nature of MARL can make it more susceptible to disruptions caused by malicious agents or unexpected behaviours from other vehicles. Unlike single‐agent systems, where robustness can be achieved through isolated adjustments, maintaining robustness in a multi‐agent setting is more complex due to the interdependence among agents."

### Adversarial Attacks on DRL
> _[Section 2.2, Related Work — Adversarial Attacks on DRL]_

"Though deep learning models recently achieve significant improvement, research shows that these well‐trained models are still very vulnerable to adversarial attacks. Adversarial attacks on camera sensor often lead to visually similar images to the normal images from a human perspective, yet they can deceive deep learning models into generating inaccurate predictions. Generally there are two types of adversarial attack methods, white‐box attacks and black‐box attacks, depending on if the attacker has full access to the models' parameters or not. In Ref. [33], a Bayes optimisation‐based approach was proposed to generate the painting of black lines on the road to counterfeit lane lines and make the vehicle deviate from the original orientation. Experiments were conducted in CARLA simulator, and results showed that end‐to‐end driving models were attacked and deviated to the orientation chosen by attackers. He et al. [34] combined Bayesian optimisation and Jensen‐Shannon (JS) divergence to measure average variation distance of the policies attacked by the observation perturbations for optimal black‐box attacks. Behzadan and Munir [35] studied black‐box attacks on DQNs with discrete actions via transferability of adversarial examples. Pattanaik et al. [36] further enhanced adversarial attacks to DRL with multi‐step gradient descent and better engineered loss function. They required a critic or Q function to perform attacks. Typically, the critic network learnt during agent training. For white‐box approaches, S. Huang et al. [37] evaluated the robustness of deep reinforcement learning policies through an FGSM based attack on Atari games with discrete actions. Kos and Song [38] proposed to use the value function to guide adversarial perturbation search. Y. C. Lin et al. [39] considered a more complicated case where the adversary is allowed to attack only a subset of time steps, and used a generative model to generate attack plans luring the agent to a designated target state. In our work, we introduce a FGSM based method to generate optimal adversary examples by maximising the pre‐defined collision risk. Results show that with a small strength parameter ε and minimal visual difference, our method can efficiently misguide the well‐trained agent."

### Mitigation Against Adversarial Attacks
> _[Section 2.3, Related Work — Mitigation Against Adversarial Attacks]_

"Defence methods against adversarial attacks have been explored recently. Zhang, Chen, Xiao et al. [40] proposed a novel Markov decision process (SA‐MDP) that considers state‐adversarial perturbations and provides a theoretical foundation for robust single‐agent reinforcement learning. They developed the principle of policy regularisation that can possibly be applied to many DRL algorithms. Based on SA‐MDP, Zhang, Chen, Boning et al. [41] proposed an alternative training framework with learnt adversaries and developed a robust Markov game to address environmental uncertainty by introducing uncertainty into the reward function. Oikarinen et al. [42] proposed the robust ADversarIAl loss (RADIALRL) method, which can improve the robustness of DRL within the ℓp norm boundary against attacks with lower computational complexity. Kumar et al. [43] proposed certified robustness by adding smoothing noise to the state. However, these methods are designed for single‐agent RL systems and overlook the specific challenges of MARL, making them difficult to apply effectively. MARL systems are often more vulnerable to adversarial attacks, even when only a single agent is targeted [44]. To counter state‐based attacks, Zhou et al. [13] proposed robust policies by minimising the cross‐entropy loss between the actions of agents in nonperturbed and perturbed states. Compared to previous methods, our work focuses on mitigating perturbations in both local agent states and global shared states, addressing a more challenging problem than prior approaches."

### From the Introduction (single-agent robust RL background)
> _[Introduction]_

"Recognising the importance of model robustness, researches have investigated the single‐agent DRL. Several works [16, 17] adopt adversarial training from supervised learning scheme to improve the robustness of such learning‐based guidance schemes. Specifically, the agent is occasionally attacked and the adversarial trajectories are generated during the data collection. However, these data augmentation style training with adversarial samples only brings limited improvement. Moreover, comparing to robust single‐agent algorithms, enabling robustness for multi‐agent systems faces more challenges, as not only the driving agents are influenced by the adversarial attacks, but their behaviours after the attacks could affect other agents in the same scenario."

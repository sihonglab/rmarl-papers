## Taxonomy
- **Robustness / perturbation type targeted**: State/observation perturbation (ϵ-bounded ℓ∞ adversarial perturbations of agents' observations) in cooperative MARL; adversarial training against an observation adversary
- **Method paradigm**: Adversarial training with a stochastic adversary; factorized maximum-entropy (soft) MARL; policy-adversarial actor-director decomposition (director + generator); CTDE value decomposition
- **Keywords**: Robust MARL, observation perturbation, adversarial training, stochastic adversary, maximum entropy, CTDE

## TL;DR
The paper proposes Adversarial Training with Stochastic Adversary (ATSA), in which a stochastic adversary — composed of a Stochastic Director (SDor) that suggests reward-minimizing, entropy-maximizing policy perturbations and an SDor-guided generaTor (STor) that produces the corresponding observation perturbations — is trained online with the protagonist agents, together with a new SDor-STor loss that aligns the two, yielding MARL models robust to diverse observation perturbations without overfitting to the strongest adversary.

## Problem & Motivation
MARL models are sensitive to perturbations in observations, lowering their trustworthiness in complex environments such as autonomous driving. Adversarial training can improve robustness, but existing methods (1) overfit to adversarial perturbations of observations, losing performance on clean observations, and (2) fail to incorporate prior information about the protagonist's policy, causing misalignment between the actor (which generates observation perturbations) and the director (which suggests policy perturbations), leading to training instability. Directly transplanting single-agent adversarial training to MARL is hard because the adversary's high-dimensional action space (the observation space) makes RL-based adversaries difficult to train, one-step misclassification does not equate to minimized long-term reward, and a per-agent adversary must consider its impact on team rewards.

## Robustness Setting
- **Threat model / uncertainty set**: An observation adversary perturbs each protagonist agent i's observation within an ϵ-bounded ℓ∞ ball Bⁱ_ϵ = {ôⁱ ∈ Oⁱ : ‖ôⁱ − oⁱ‖_∞ ≤ ϵ}. Formalized as OD-POMDP (observation-adversarial Dec-POMDP) and PD-POMDP (policy-adversarial Dec-POMDP), where observation perturbations are shown to be equivalent to policy perturbations (following Sun et al., 2022b). The adversary's reward is the negative of the protagonist team reward; the adversary is stochastic (soft constraints, not always the strongest).
- **Setting**: Cooperative MARL; CTDE; online adversarial training; discrete action space and deterministic protagonist policies.

## Method
- Models the adversary problem as a PD-POMDP viewed as a probabilistic graphical model (control-as-inference, following Levine 2018), introducing an optimality variable Xt and defining SDor's objective as a maximum-entropy objective: maximize expected adversary reward (negative team reward) plus an entropy term with temperature α (Definition 3.1).
- SDor (Stochastic Director): learns a factorized soft policy under CTDE using factorized maximum-entropy MARL (FOP, Zhang et al. 2021b). The optimal joint and individual soft policies take exponential (softmax) form in their soft Q/value functions (Propositions 3.2, 3.3); learning alternates joint soft policy evaluation and individual soft policy improvement (Theorem 3.4), with TD-error losses for the soft-Q / value / weight networks and a policy-gradient update for individual policies.
- STor (SDor-guided generaTor): given SDor's optimal policy, generates adversarial observations that drive the deterministic protagonist to act according to SDor's suggestions, by minimizing a KL divergence between the protagonist's perturbed action distribution and SDor's policy; in practice solved with FGSM-based gradient steps (clipped to the valid range).
- SDor-STor loss: a new cross-entropy loss measuring the gap between the policy perturbation suggested by SDor and that implemented through STor; added to SDor's individual policy objective with weight κ to keep STor and SDor coherent.

## Theoretical Contributions
- Proves (via factorized maximum-entropy MARL) that SDor's factorized soft policy iteration converges to a global optimum (Theorem 3.4, with Lemmas E.1–E.2).
- Proves optimality of SDor-STor (Theorem 3.5): an optimal joint soft policy of SDor in the PD-POMDP, combined with STor, induces an optimal stochastic observation adversary against the protagonist in the OD-POMDP (proof via an intermediate policy-perturbation Dec-POMDP, Lemmas F.2–F.3).
- Provides a time-complexity analysis of the ATSA training procedure (Theorem G.1).

## Experiments
- **Environment/Benchmark**: StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge (SMAC) — maps 3m, 3s_3z, 8m; and a Connected and Autonomous Vehicles (CAV) environment (three autonomous vehicles plus 1–4 human-driven ones). Continuous-action experiments (MPE predator-prey with MADDPG/FACMAC) in the appendix.
- **Baselines**: Implemented on VDN and QMIX protagonists. Adversaries/methods: NoAdv, Random Noise (RN), FGSM, ATLA, PAAD; robust-learning baselines PR, PR-REP, ERNIE; stochastic-adversary baselines RAP and ROMANCE-p/s.
- **Evaluation metrics**: SMAC — Win Rate (WR), averaged over six adversaries (AVG); CAV — average cumulative reward and Crash Rate (CR); statistical significance via Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Perturbations are ℓ∞-bounded (0.25 for 3m/3s_3z, 0.1 for 8m, 0.05 for CAV); no adversary retraining per cell.

## Key Results
- ATSA achieves the highest average win rate across the SMAC scenarios, outperforming 11 baselines across 3 scenarios with 42 statistically significant wins; it maintains stable or improved performance in perturbation-free and random-noise settings, where FGSM/PAAD-trained models degrade.
- In CAV, ATSA surpasses most baselines in both cumulative reward and crash rate, maintaining robust performance under diverse adversaries.
- Ablation: removing the SDor-STor loss (κ = 0) markedly lowers win rate, especially under PAAD and ATSA adversaries, confirming the loss is crucial for SDor-STor consistency and stable adversary training; as α → 0 the policy becomes deterministic and robustness declines (resembling PAAD).
- Attack analysis: because ATSA's adversaries are stochastic with soft constraints, they are not always the strongest, but they produce more diverse perturbations; FGSM-trained models that resist FGSM attacks degrade sharply under ATSA attacks, indicating FGSM-based training overfits its own perturbation patterns.

## Limitations & Future Work
- Designed for discrete action spaces and deterministic protagonist policies; appendix experiments show limited gains in continuous action spaces (e.g., weak under FACMAC), attributed to the difficulty of inducing coordinated worst-case behavior via gradient-based attacks and generating compound step-wise perturbations.
- Future work: extend the framework to continuous-action algorithms; explore model-based RL that treats protagonist policies and environment as a unified system so the adversary can fully leverage protagonist information; apply ATSA to broader real-world systems.

## Relevance to Survey
This paper sits on the "state/observation-perturbation robustness" line of robust MARL and the "adversarial training" method line. It extends the single-agent SA-MDP / policy-adversarial actor-director (PAAD) paradigm to cooperative MARL and introduces a stochastic (maximum-entropy) adversary to combat overfitting to the strongest adversary, connecting robust SARL (SA-MDP, ATLA, PAAD), factorized maximum-entropy MARL (FOP), and stochastic-adversary policy-robustness work (RAP, ROMANCE). It is a useful reference for the distinction between policy robustness and state robustness in multi-agent settings.

## Related Work (verbatim excerpts from the paper)

> _[Appendix A.2, Robust Single-Agent Reinforcement Learning]_

"In the field of SARL, improving model robustness relies on two approaches: robust learning and adversarial training. The works in (Liang et al., 2022; Oikarinen et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2020) are currently the most representative method in robust learning. Zhang et al. (Zhang et al., 2020) develop a policy regularization principle that can be broadly applied to various SARL algorithms. In addition, the State-Adversarial Markov Decision Process (SA-MDP) framework incorporates adversarial perturbations in states, providing a theoretical foundation for robust SARL. In constructing the adversarial regularization term, Liang et al. (Liang et al., 2022) consider not only adversarial perturbations of states but also the significance of the current state within the entire episode. This approach enables the policy network to focus more on the performance of critical states during updates, further enhancing the model's robustness. Unlike the previous two methods, the work in (Oikarinen et al., 2021) employs the concept of interval-bound propagation to construct a regularization term. This approach enhances model robustness by optimizing the boundaries of the decision network. While robust learning can enhance a model's robustness, models trained this way are still sensitive to more potent attacks. Yang et al. (Yang et al., 2024) improves the robustness of reinforcement learning systems by leveraging monitoring mechanisms based on probabilistic automata to guide real-time correction of critical actions . Zhang et al. (Zhang et al., 2021a) use reinforcement learning to Alternate Training protagonist agents with Learned Adversaries (ATLA). This approach performs well in scenarios with small observation spaces. However, as the agent's observation space grows, leading to a larger action space for the adversary, training the adversary model becomes challenging. Sun et al. (Sun et al., 2022b) demonstrate that observation perturbations are equivalent to policy perturbations. Based on this theoretical foundation, the Policy Adversarial Actor and Director (PAAD) is proposed, decomposing the solution of the observation adversary into two steps: the director determines the strongest policy perturbation, and then the actor generates the optimal observation perturbation based on this policy perturbation. This approach effectively addresses the issues present in ATLA. PAAD aims to enhance a model's performance against the strongest adversaries. However, this approach may lead to overfitting to these adversarial perturbations of observations, thereby reducing performance on clean ones."

> _[Appendix A.3, Robust Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning]_

"In the multi-agent domain, most existing studies primarily focus on action manipulation (Li et al., 2019; 2023; Sun et al., 2022a), policy-level attacks (Guo et al., 2022; Reddi et al., 2024), or adversarial communication (Sun et al., 2023). Other works adopt domain randomization to address the sim-to-real gap (Chen et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2023). In contrast, relatively little attention has been paid to adversarial perturbations on observations. By extending SA-MDP to multi-agent fields, Zhou et al. (Zhou et al., 2024c) propose a state-adversarial stochastic game and discuss its properties and propose a robust training framework based on mean-field actor-critic (Yang et al., 2018), which includes an action loss based on the difference in action distributions between clean and perturbation-free observations, along with the iterative regularization method for action loss. However, this method falls within the scope of robust learning and is also sensitive to stronger adversaries. Guo et al. (Guo et al., 2024; 2025) extend SARL robustness techniques (Goodfellow et al., 2014; Sun et al., 2022b; Zhang et al., 2020; 2021a) to multi-agent scenarios, where multi-agent PAAD achieves notable results. However, it tends to overfit to the perturbation generated by the strongest adversary, compromising performance in other situations. Stochastic adversary has been studied in policy robustness in some papers, such as ROMANCE (Yuan et al., 2023b) and RAP (Vinitsky et al., 2020). However, there are significant differences between policy robustness and state robustness in terms of both formulation and motivation. Formally, policy robustness assumes that the adversary directly perturbs the agent's actions, modifying the output of the policy. In contrast, state robustness introduces perturbations to the agent's input states. This fundamental difference in formulation directly leads to distinct objectives. In multi-agent settings, policy robustness aims to ensure that the overall system performance remains stable even when some agents' decisions are perturbed. To facilitate stable training under such settings, methods like ROMANCE (Yuan et al., 2023b) introduce a sparse action attack budget to limit the number of adversarial interventions, thereby preserving controllability. In contrast, state robustness focuses on enabling each agent to make correct decisions even when its input states are perturbed. The goal is to make every agent robust to state perturbation. Therefore, the sparse action perturbation constraints used in ROMANCE (Yuan et al., 2023b) are not applicable to state perturbations."

> _[Introduction]_

"Existing studies (Zhang et al., 2020; 2021a) prove that training an adversary to generate an adversarial perturbation of observation can be modeled as a Markov decision process, in which the reward received by the adversary is the negative counterpart of the reward received by the protagonist agent, and its action space is the observation space of the protagonist agent. Then they use reinforcement learning to train an adversary. However, high-dimensional action space in reinforcement learning presents great implementation difficulties. The work in (Sun et al., 2022b) attempts to address these challenges by training adversaries by using policy adversarial actor and director. The latter provides suggestions for policy perturbations of the protagonist agent to achieve its worst-case performance, while the former generates an adversarial perturbation of observation."

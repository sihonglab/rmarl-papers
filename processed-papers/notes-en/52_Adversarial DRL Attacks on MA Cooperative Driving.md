# 52. Adversarial Deep Reinforcement Learning Attacks on Multi-Agent Autonomous Cooperative Driving Policies

## Metadata
- **Title**: Adversarial Deep Reinforcement Learning Attacks on Multi-Agent Autonomous Cooperative Driving Policies
- **Authors**: Ahmed Alzubaidi, Ameena S. Al-Sumaiti, Majid Khonji
- **Affiliation**: Khalifa University, Abu Dhabi, UAE (Al Nokhba Program & Smart OR Lab, Advanced Power and Energy Center, Department of Electrical Engineering; KUCARS Center, Department of Computer Science)
- **Venue**: IET Intelligent Transport Systems 2025 (Vol. 19, e70066)
- **Link/arXiv**: https://doi.org/10.1049/itr2.70066

## Taxonomy
- **Robustness / perturbation type targeted**: Adversarial agents / environment-manipulation attacks (a malicious adversary vehicle co-exists in the environment with cooperative agents); robustness of cooperative-driving MARL policies to adversarial behavior at deployment
- **Method paradigm**: Adversarial DRL (A-DRL) for attack generation; single-agent A2C-trained adversary policies; adversarial training as defense (homogeneous, heterogeneous, curriculum learning)
- **Keywords**: Adversarial attacks, cooperative driving, connected autonomous vehicles (CAVs), MARL, adversarial training, on-ramp merging

## TL;DR
The paper shows that MARL-trained cooperative driving (CD-MARL) policies for connected autonomous vehicles are vulnerable to a single adversarial vehicle via environment-manipulation black-box attacks (a collision adversary and a speed adversary), and proposes adversarial-training defenses that substantially improve safety against the seen adversary but fail to generalize to unseen adversaries.

## Problem & Motivation
MARL is increasingly used to train cooperative decision models for connected autonomous vehicles (CAVs), but like all deep learning models these policies inherit vulnerability to adversarial attacks. The authors argue that no prior work has explored the adversarial-attack security concern specifically for MARL-trained cooperative driving policies. They aim to (RQ-1) determine whether deployed CD-MARL policies in a fully autonomous cooperative environment are vulnerable to adversarial attacks, and (RQ-2) if so, what defense strategies can improve robustness. The threat is realistic because an adversary car can plausibly share the road environment, whereas sensor/actuator (observation/action) manipulation is assumed to be precluded by proper security measures.

## Robustness Setting
- **Threat model / uncertainty set**: Environment Manipulation attack — an adversary CAV (CAVa) is introduced into an on-ramp merge scenario populated by victim CAVs running policy πvc; regular CAVs cannot distinguish the adversary from regular CAVs. The adversary performs black-box attacks (zero knowledge of the deployed model and weights), with capabilities limited to executing driving actions in the shared environment (no access to other vehicles' sensors/actuators). Restricted to active attacks. Two adversarial objectives: collision adversary (advc) maximizing collisions, and speed adversary (advs) minimizing neighbors' average speed.
- **Setting**: Victim is cooperative (CTDE-trained MA2C, parameter-sharing, POMDP); the adversary is trained as a single-agent MDP (single-agent A2C). Online training in simulation (Highway-env). Attack at deployment/test time of the victim policy.

## Method
- **Victim policy (πvc)**: An existing CD-MARL on-ramp merging policy by Chen et al. [11], trained with parameter-sharing multi-agent advantage actor-critic (MA2C) under CTDE; environment modeled as a POMDP ({Ai, Si, Ri}i∈V, T); per-agent reward combines safety (rc), speed efficiency (rs), headway (rh), and merge cost (rm), with global (Gt) and regional (Ri,t) reward notions; coupled with a priority-based safety supervisor.
- **Adversary design**: Single adversary CAVa formulated as an MDP (S, A, R, T). State St = {PR, PO, SP} encodes presence, relative positions, and relative speeds of n neighbors. Action space A = {turnLeft, idle, turnRight, speedUp, speedDown}, identical to victim CAVs, with an action frequency of 5 (every 0.2 s) and a mask layer zeroing invalid-action probabilities.
- **Adversary reward**: Collision-attacker advc uses Rcoll = sum over neighbors of crashed indicator (Eq. 10); speed-attacker advs uses Rspeed = sum over neighbors of −rsi (Eq. 12), i.e., minimizing neighbors' speed-efficiency reward.
- **Training architecture**: Single-agent A2C with per-element FC encoders, concatenation, then a shared FC, feeding actor (πΘ, 128-neuron FC + softmax of length 5) and critic (V, 128-neuron FC) networks; simulated in Highway-env with 6 CAVs (one adversary, five victims), same setup/initialization as [11].
- **Defense (adversarial training)**: Retrain πvc in adversary-injected environments to obtain a new policy π. Three approaches: (i) Homogeneous (πhmc against advc; πhms against advs), (ii) Heterogeneous (πht, attacker selected randomly each episode with equal weight), (iii) Curriculum Learning (two phases by adversary type — πclc first attacked by advc, πcls first attacked by advs).

## Theoretical Contributions
None / mostly empirical.

## Experiments
- **Environment/Benchmark**: On-ramp merge scenarios simulated in Highway-env [40], using the same setup/initialization as Chen et al. [11]; 6 CAVs total (5 victims + 1 trainable adversary). Three evaluation environments: adv-fr-env (no adversary), adv-co-env (collision adversary advc), adv-sp-env (speed adversary advs).
- **Baselines**: The undefended victim policy πvc (the existing CD-MARL policy of Chen et al. [11]); defended policies compared among themselves (πhmc, πhms, πht, πclc, πcls).
- **Evaluation metrics**: Cumulative Global Reward (cg), Cumulative Regional Reward (cr), Collision-Free Rate (co), Time to Collision (tc), Average Speed (avg). Results averaged over three separately trained policies, each evaluated over 30 episodes of 20 s.

## Key Results
- The adversaries significantly degrade πvc: against advc the collision rate ("co" reported) rose to 62% (from 0% in adv-fr-env) with cg dropping to ~26 and tc to 14.29 s; against advs cg dropped to ~47.58, co to 10%, and average speed fell from 25 m/s to 21.73 m/s. (Abstract: collision rate increased to 62% and average speed dropped from 25 m/s to 21.73 m/s.)
- Homogeneous adversarial training against the seen adversary works best: πhmc cut the collision rate against advc by more than half (62% → 28%, cg 26.01 → 54.43), and πhms eliminated collisions against advs (0% collision in adv-sp-env, cg increased by 8.18).
- Defended policies fail to generalize to unseen adversaries: πhmc could not cope with advs (43% collision) and πhms could not cope with advc (67% collision); the generalized policies (πht, πclc, πcls) did not yield a reliable robust policy, with πht the worst and even degrading performance in the adversary-free adv-fr-env.

## Limitations & Future Work
- A stated limitation is the failure to develop policies robust against unseen types of adversaries; the generalized approaches (heterogeneous, curriculum learning) did not produce a reliable robust policy.
- Even the best homogeneous defense (πhmc) leaves a 28% collision rate, which the authors note remains a safety concern; advc is argued to be capable of producing unpreventable adversarial situations.
- Future work: continued research in CAV security and on adversarial training as a defensive mechanism against attacks on CD-MARL policies.

## Relevance to Survey
This paper sits on the "adversarial agents / adversarial attacks" line of robust MARL, applied to safety-critical cooperative autonomous driving. It is an empirical attack-and-defense study: adversarial-DRL attack policies (environment manipulation, black-box) expose the brittleness of cooperative MARL policies, and adversarial training is used as the defense. It connects to the adversarial-policy literature (Gleave et al.), adversarial attacks on DRL (Behzadan, Huang, Lin), and to closely related MARL autonomous-driving robustness work (Sharif & Marijan), illustrating the robustness-vs-generalization gap (defense overfits to the seen adversary) that motivates more principled robust MARL.

## Related Work (verbatim excerpts from the paper)
> _[Section 2.1, Related Work — Adversarial DRL]_

"In this subsection, major works in the field of adversarial DRL (A-DRL) are highlighted. Behzadan et al. [31] demonstrated the vulnerability of DQN when exposed to adversarial attacks. They assumed the attacker is limited to black-box attacks, which are capable of perturbing the environment before the agent captures the current observation. An initialization phase occurs before any interaction with the victim policy (𝜋𝑣𝑐), which constitutes obtaining an adversarial DQN policy (𝜋𝑎𝑑𝑣) based on an adversarial reward function and replicating the victim DQN with random parameters. Subsequently, the execution phase starts, where the attacker selects its desired action 𝑎𝑎𝑑𝑣 based on 𝜋𝑎𝑑𝑣. Then, leveraging adversarial sample generation techniques such as FGSM and JSMA, to produce perturbations on the environment state that impose 𝑎𝑎𝑑𝑣 to be selected by 𝜋𝑣𝑐. To extend on [31] work, Huang et al. [32] have explored the vulnerability during test-time and transferability across policies and training DRL algorithms in both white-box and black-box attacks. They have also studied the vulnerability of A3C and TPRO, where a significant drop in performance was noticed as a result of the attacks, albeit outperforming DQN. Lin et al. [33] proposed strategically timed attacks that target producing adversarial attacks against DRL while minimizing the number of attacked time steps during the episode, with the claim that a uniform approach is easy to detect as done in [31, 32]. Lin et al. have also proposed a technique called enchanting attack, where the adversary aims to force the victim agent to arrive at a specific target state. This was done by exploiting video generative models and a planning algorithm that defines the perturbation inserted by the adversary to impose a particular state on the victim agent. The suggested attacks were applied on A3C and DQN. Gleave et al. [34] proposed a technique to devise an adversarial policy that attacks the victim policy using environment manipulation settings, where an adversary agent acts as an opponent competing with the victim policy in zero-sum game. The adversarial policy will be trained to maximize the adversary's opponent reward pushing to defeat the victim. Their solution was evaluated in two-player game in MuJoCo games, where the trained adversarial policy succeeded in outperforming the victim agent."

> _[Section 2.2, Related Work — AV Applications]_

"This section continues to cover the literature with a closer look at A-DRL uses in AV applications. Behzadan et al. [35] proposed a general framework to leverage A-DRL to test the performance of collision avoidance models deployed by AVs under worst-case scenarios. The adversary agent is trained with various objectives, namely, (1) causing direct collision with AV, (2) imposing the AV to collide with another vehicle, and (3) interfering with the desired path trajectory for the AV. They suggested evaluating the performance of the adversarial policy based on episodes to convergence, adversary reward, time to collision, and the amount of damage caused. Wachi [36] work has utilized A-DRL to find failure scenarios on rule-based AVs. This was achieved by having multiple adversary agents interact with a rule-based model to cause an accident or delay the arrival of the AV to the desired destination. It was argued that a multi-adversary attack approach will lead to a broad range of scenarios that can be used to improve the state of the rule-based algorithm. He et al. [37] developed robust LCD models to counteract the noise from inaccurate measurement readings. These observational perturbations were generated through black-box adversarial attacks using a Bayesian optimization approach. A constrained observation-robust A2C algorithm was subsequently designed and trained to operate in AVs. In their later work, the same team [37] maintained the targeted LCD but employed white-box adversarial attacks instead. Sharif et al. [38] have demonstrated the vulnerability of MARL-trained driving policies against adversarial attacks generated by an adversary. The considered environment in the study was a non-signalized T-intersection consisting of two CAVs and an adversarial AV. Two adversaries were trained with distinct adversarial objectives which are causing collision and imposing an offroad steering. To address the discovered vulnerability by the adversaries, an adversarial training defense mechanism was utilized to improve the robustness of MARL-trained policies. Despite their success in adversarial training in terms of tangible reduction in offroad steering, the MARL-trained policy failed to mitigate collision occurrence with 25% of testing runs failing to be collision-free. From the literature, it was found that no prior work has explored the adversarial attacks security concern in regards to MARL-trained cooperative driving policies. As a result, the primary motivation of this work is to fill this research gap."

Abstract
Robust multi-robot behaviour hinges on more than network archi-
tecture: it critically depends on what the task punishes. The MAPPO
algorithm has been analyzed on two canonical benchmarks that
share a navigation objective but differ in their reward shaping.
In Navigation, where collisions incur penalties, performance col-
lapses once a critical fraction of agents is faulty, even under mild
sensor noise. In Sampling, where collisions are ignored, the pol-
icy withstands swarm-wide degradation until the magnitude of
observation noise exceeds the radius of the hotspot, after which the
reward plummets uniformly for every agent ratio. The results indi-
cate that the algorithm exhibits some robustness to certain types
of perturbations, primarily due to the generalization properties of
the neural networks, but this robustness is strongly shaped by the
structure of the reward function. Three design lessons emerge: (i)
reward shaping decides which disturbance axis is fatal; (ii) every
task has a tolerance window where moderate degradation is harm-
less; (iii) failures are dominated by a handful of topologically central
“hub” robots, so identifying and shielding these critical agents offers
high leverage. Taken together, our findings chart a path towards
resilient real-world deployments: combine collision-aware rewards
with targeted protection of influence-maximising agents.

While multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) has demon-

most existing studies assume ideal operational conditions. However,

This work focuses on analyzing the robustness of a MARL al-
gorithm under scenarios where one or more agents suffer partial
impairments. Controlled fault injection mechanisms are applied
during the evaluation phase, simulating realistic degradations in
perception capabilities. The goal is to assess how well Multi-Agent
Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO) [15] can maintain coopera-
tive performance when some teammates are affected by varying
levels of observation noise. Both the severity of the noise and the
proportion of agents affected are considered.
The contributions of the paper are as follows:
• A systematic fault injection framework is introduced tailored
for multi-agent reinforcement learning environments.
• The state-of-the-art MAPPO algorithm is benchmarked un-
der varying degrees of agent impairments.
• Empirical insights are provided with respect to the resilience
of MAPPO and directions for enhancing robustness against
partial failures are suggested.
2
Related Work
A growing body of research probes the fragility of cooperative
multi-agent reinforcement learning under intentionally crafted
disturbances. Most existing studies focus on targeted, gradient-based
attacks that perturb the local state of agents so as to maximise team-
level disruption. Comprehensive test suites [8] systematically inject
adversarial perturbations into observation and reward channels,
while other works [9, 17] demonstrate that corrupting only a few
strategically chosen agents can trigger group-wide failure.
Agent drop-outs and communication losses are modelled in some
approaches [16], while others [6] crafts Gaussian noise with pre-
defined distribution profiles to mimic sensor drift. Although earlier
studies [5, 11] acknowledge stochastic disturbances, their coverage
of noise types and magnitudes remains limited.
The understanding of worst-case, attacker-driven scenarios is
advanced, but how naturally occurring sensor noise and partial

3
Methodology
To investigate the robustness of multi-agent systems with a high
number of agents, an evaluation pipeline was designed that intro-
duces perturbations only during the evaluation phase.
3.1
Training Phase
Agents are trained under standard procedures in a stable, noise-free
environment. This setup ensures that learning is based solely on
clean observations, allowing agents to develop optimal policies
without external disturbances.
To monitor learning progress, periodic evaluation points are
introduced throughout the training phase. Each evaluation consists
of multiple episodes to capture reliable performance metrics. These
evaluations are carried out both in the original, unperturbed en-
vironment and in settings where observation noise is introduced,
enabling the assessment of robustness alongside baseline perfor-
mance.
In addition, training is repeated across several independent runs.
This approach improves the reliability of the findings by averaging
out variability and ensuring that reported results reflect consistent
learning behavior rather than random fluctuations [1].
3.2
Evaluation Phase with Perturbations
During the evaluation phase, noisy evaluation environments are
included alongside the standard unperturbed setting. In these en-
vironments, agents are exposed to disturbances not encountered
during training, simulating conditions such as partial failures or
sensor degradation.
For each noisy evaluation scenario:
• A subset of agents is selected, representing a certain percent-
age of the total population to be affected by perturbations.
• Gaussian noise is introduced to the observations, received
by the selected agents (see Equation 1). Different noise levels
are applied to simulate varying severity of faults.

zero-mean normal distribution with configurable standard devia-

3.4
Performance Measurement
Since the focus is on cooperative multi-agent tasks, system perfor-
mance is measured based on global reward (i.e., a shared common
goal). Average global reward obtained under clean and noisy eval-
uation scenarios are compared, quantifying the impact of partial
agent failures.
4
Experiments
To empirically evaluate the robustness against partial agent fail-
ures of the investigated algorithm, a series of experiments were
conducted on two cooperative multi-agent tasks under varying
levels of noise and agent impairments. The key components of the
experimental setup are detailed in the following sections.
4.1
Algorithm and Training Framework
The Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization algorithm is utilised
as the learning backbone for all experiments. MAPPO is a widely
used, actor-critic-based policy optimization method tailored for
cooperative multi-agent settings, extending the original PPO algo-
rithm to multi-agent scenarios.
Agents are trained under a Centralized Training with Decentral-
ized Execution (CTDE) framework [2], where policies are condi-
tioned only on local observations during execution but benefit from
centralized information during training. This setup enables efficient
training while ensuring that policies can operate independently in
deployment.
The implementation is based on TorchRL [4], leveraging its built-
in support for multi-agent communication, vectorized environ-
ments, and scalable policy optimization.
4.2
Environments and Tasks
The evaluation tasks are selected from the Vectorized Multi-Agent
Simulator [3], a lightweight and fast physics-based simulator de-
signed for multi-agent learning research. Two cooperative tasks
were selected that rely on a common reward structure:
Navigation. Each agent is assigned a randomly generated goal
location within an unbounded continuous two-dimensional space
(see Figure 1, top row). Agents must navigate towards their re-
spective targets while avoiding collisions with other agents. The
action space is continuous, allowing fine-grained control over move-
ment. Agents have access to their relative position to their goal and
proximity-based collision sensors. The success of the task depends
on all agents reaching their targets safely. As a baseline, a heuristic
solution, provided by the environment, is employed with a minimal
adaptation that incorporates lidar sensor feedback. This improves
collision avoidance by adjusting the straight-line goal direction
away from nearby obstacles.
Sampling. Agents are deployed in a workspace shaped by a mul-
timodal Gaussian density field (see Figure 1, bottom row). Their
objective is to explore the environment and collect samples dis-
tributed across the discretized grid. Each sample is retrieved when
an agent visits its corresponding cell. Rewards are either shared
among all agents or assigned individually, depending on the reward
setting. Agents receive local observations, including nearby sample
values. They can also detect teammates using lidar sensors. The task
encourages spatial coverage, coordination, and efficient exploration
of high-density regions. In this scenario, a heuristic baseline was
developed that guides agents directly towards regions of highest
sampling value while actively avoiding collisions.
Both tasks operate in continuous action and observation spaces
under full observability. They require non-trivial coordination among

Hyperparameter Configurations
Training was conducted with multiple hyperparameter configura-

4.4
Evaluation
The evaluation protocol applied follows the standard proposed by
Gorsane et al. [7]. Each point on a learning curve (see Figure 2)
corresponds to the episode-mean reward averaged across a batch of
evaluation episodes, with the shaded band denoting the associated
standard deviation. For each task, five fully independent training
runs were conducted that differed only in their random seeds but
shared the hyperparameter settings (see Table 1). During each run,
the agent team was trained exclusively in a clean, unperturbed
environment. At regular intervals training was paused and the
policy was evaluated under all perturbation configurations using
independent evaluation episodes. The full grid of these perturbation
settings is visualized in Figures 3 and 5.

Navigation Task
Overall trend. Performance is far more sensitive to how many
agents are degraded than to how strongly they are degraded. When
≤30% of the team is affected, MAPPO retains at least 70% of
its clean–evaluation reward even at the largest noise level tested
(𝜎= 4.0). Conversely, once at least half of the agents are impaired,
the global reward declines almost linearly with the number of faulty
teammates, reaching a relative change of −144% when every agent
is disturbed, as shown on Figure 4.
Sparse faults (≤20% faulty). A small minority of bad actors is
almost fully absorbed by the team. Even under the harshest noise
(𝜎= 4.0) the swarm retains over half of its baseline reward: 𝑅≈72
with 0% faulty and 𝑅≈49 with 10% faulty. The reward remains
comfortably positive throughout the 𝜎range and the standard

neighbors quickly correct the path of a stray agent and collisions

Partial faults (30–60% faulty). Once roughly one third of the

still manages modest forward progress (𝑅= 41 at 30% faulty) but
the mean crosses zero near the 40–50% mark and turns negative

Variance. As the proportion of affected agents increases along
the Y-axis, the variance of aggregated system returns also grows
markedly. This trend is especially visible from 50% upwards, where
the standard deviation of the return can exceed 8.7, particularly in
the high-noise range. This suggests that the behavior of the system
becomes increasingly unpredictable not just as more agents are
perturbed, but depending on which specific agents are impaired. For
instance, even when the noise magnitude is kept constant, runs
with the same noise level and agent impairment ratio can differ by
more than 40% in return, depending on the agents involved.
This highlights that, despite a homogeneous agent design, cer-
tain agents play disproportionately critical roles—particularly those
located within the core of agent clusters (in this particular scenario).
When such central agents exhibit faulty or noisy behavior, their
suboptimal actions cascade through the group by coordination
breakdowns, resulting in substantial reward loss. Thus, the robust-
ness of the system is not only a function of the number of perturbed

Conclusion
Core finding. Robustness of MAPPO is task–dependent. In Nav-
igation, reward collapses when many robots fail, whereas in Sam-
pling it collapses when observation noise becomes large, no matter
how many robots are affected. This flip is driven by the reward
design—collision penalties in Navigation amplify inter-agent inter-
ference, while their absence in Sampling lets the policy tolerate
even swarm-wide degradation until the noise displaces everyone
from the hotspot.
Why any robustness appears. Both policies share the same
neural backbone, which absorbs light perturbations and degrades
gracefully up to task-specific breakpoints. Beyond those points,
however, neither network can compensate unaided, revealing the
limit of “free” robustness obtained from standard training.
Architectural implications. Reward shaping matters: what
the task punishes determines which disturbance axis is fatal. Tol-
erance windows exist: moderate degradation can be harmless
if it stays within task-specific limits. Critical agents dominate
failure: runs where centrally located ’hub’ robots are corrupt incur
the highest losses. Shielding such agents, yields high leverage.
Future directions. When an algorithm is intended for use in the
physical world, its training environment should reflect real-world
constraints—especially the need to avoid collisions. This argues for
reward functions that explicitly penalise unsafe contacts. Such that
the learned policy behaves more like the Navigation model and less
like the collision-free Sampling variant.
A promising next step is to identify “critical” agents: robots
whose position or communication role means their failure rever-
berates through the team. They may receive special attention, for
example, by receiving redundant sensors or backup controllers. Ad-
ditionally, studying the behavior of the system when only a certain
percentage of agents receive degraded or noisy observations can
offer insight into how partial perceptual corruption impacts global
performance. By combining task-realistic penalties with targeted
protection of such agents, future work can push MAPPO from
the graceful-but-limited robustness of today toward the level of
reliability demanded by real multi-robot deployments.

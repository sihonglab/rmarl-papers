ABSTRACT
In recent years, multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) has been increasingly applied in training cooperative decision models
for connected autonomous vehicles (CAVs). Despite the success they have demonstrated, they are bound to inherit issues that
deep learning models suffer, such as vulnerability to adversarial attacks which is the focus of this study. Consequently, this paper
aims to assess and enhance the robustness of MARL-trained cooperative policies used by CAVs, in terms of their resilience to
adversarial behavior encountered during deployment. First, a specific existing cooperative policy was identified to be the victim
policy, deployed in an on-ramp merging road scenario. Second, two adversarial policies, namely collision adversary (𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑐) and
speed adversary (𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑠), were developed and trained to disrupt the performance of the victim policy. The adversarial policies
significantly impacted the victim policy, increasing the collision rate to 62% and decreasing the average speed from 25 m/s to
21.73 m/s. Finally, several adversarial training approaches were developed, producing more robust cooperative policies against
adversarial scenarios, by significantly bolstering road safety in adversarial conditions. The collision rate was cut by half against
𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑐, whereas, 0% collision scored in the face of 𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑠.

Introduction
The advent of connected autonomous vehicles (CAVs) on roads
is inevitable with an evident growth in research interests and
investment in automated transportation systems [1, 2]. CAVs
are a subset of autonomous vehicles (AV), that possess the
capability to communicate with other CAVs and infrastructure.
This communication, enabled by vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) and
vehicle-to-infrastructure (V2I) technologies [3, 4], facilitates a
cooperative road environment. In such environments, CAVs
can collaborate, negotiate, and coordinate decision-making by
sharing information such as intent and sensory data [5]. The
discussed cooperative environment has all CAVs acting toward
a shared goal that considers the interests and welfare of all
vehicles on the road. Contrary to this environment, is known as a
competitive environment where each CAV prioritizes its interests,
potentially affecting the global goal. Cooperative driving, enabled
by CAVs, aims to maximize the global utilities and benefits for
road users. The intended objective of cooperative driving is to
maximize global road metrics, such as average speed, flow rate,
and safety. The potential of cooperative driving can be exploited
in various road scenarios. For instance, traffic intersections and
ramp merging bring the rise of scenario-specific metrics such as
queue length, travel time, and average throughput [5].
An existing technique to obtain a cooperative driving behavior
is multi-agent deep reinforcement learning (MARL) which is a
generalized concept found in deep reinforcement learning (DRL)

[6, 7], moving from single-agent problem to multiple agents
settings [8, 9]. Inherited from DRL, MARL will produce driving
policies that can be deployed by cooperative CAVs in a centralized
or decentralized scheme [8, 9]. MARL has been utilized in
different cooperative driving problems such as motion planning
and trajectory prediction applied in various road scenarios.
Examples of scenarios considered are highway driving, merging
highways, intersections, and roundabouts. The growth of MARL
algorithms has produced a range of MARL algorithms, such as
MADQN, MAPPO, MAA2C, and MADDPG. These algorithms
demonstrated their success in various applications [10]. Refer-
ences [11–19] are existing papers in the literature where MARL
algorithms were leveraged in the field of autonomous driving.
Advancement in AV development has benefited significantly
from the integration of machine learning (ML) algorithms, as
they are being utilized in perception tasks and motion planning
that is imperative for the reliable operation of AVs [20]. ML
has demonstrated its effectiveness in numerous applications
and sectors where decision-making is required, such as vision
understanding [21], disease detection [22], and stock prediction
[23]. Despite this tremendous success, trained ML models pose
security concerns as they are vulnerable to adversarial attacks
[24–27]. The purpose of the attack could be to gain sensitive
information about the attacked application where the model
is operating. Another existing adversarial attack purpose is to
simply disrupt the regular behavior of the victim ML model,
which is known as an active attack [27]. In this research study,
the scope is restricted to active attacks. Typically, an adversarial
attacker executes the attack by corrupting or perturbing the input
to the model, producing what is called an adversarial sample.
Attacks are also classified based on that attacker’s knowledge,
making it either black-box attack or white-box attack [26, 27]. The
former refers to when the attacker has limited knowledge and
interaction with the victim model. While in the latter attacks,
the attacker will have an entire knowledge of the model, such as
parameters and training data, which are used to assess the model
in worst-case scenarios.
DRL is a subset of ML, thus DRL is bound to suffer from the
discussed adversarial attacks [28–30]. The adversarial attacks
against DRL agents are classified based on the component of
the Markov decision problem (MDP) that is being attacked [29].
Regarding action space, the adversary induces perturbations in
the selected action by the agent. This attack requires controlling
the actuators used by the agent. Moving to the reward function,
over the training an adversary can compromise the component
responsible for returning the reward feedback, tailoring the
feedback to achieve its adversarial objective. Last, the state space
is susceptible to two different attack methods:
∙Observation Manipulation the attacker is located between the
sensors and the agent. The attacker’s goal is to corrupt and
perturb readings obtained from the sensors. In turn, this
corrupts the constructed state that the agent receives, which
aims to impose a specific desired action to be selected by that
agent.
∙Environment Manipulation the attacker coexists with the
agent in the same environment. The adversary can manip-
ulate the environment to look for adversarial samples that
demonstrate existing vulnerabilities in the agent model.
As this paper considers the use of DRL agents within the
field of autonomous driving, one can infer that all of the
attacks mentioned earlier are impractical, except for environment
manipulation. This is because it can be assumed that controlling
sensors and actuators is impossible [29]. However, environment
manipulation is a more realistic attack, as it is plausible for an
adversary agent to share the environment. Therefore, this work
assumes that proper security measures are in place to eliminate
the risk of observation and action manipulation attacks. In the
context of cooperative CAVs that were trained using MARL,
an adversary car can exist among them with the objective of
producing adversarial behavior intended to minimize the benefits
of deploying a cooperative driving MARL-trained (CD-MARL)
policy. The adversary aims to find adversarial scenarios in which
the deployed CD-MARL is vulnerable and where safe driving can
be compromised. This paper concerns itself with the following
research questions:
∙RQ-1 Are CD-MARL policies deployed in CAVs in a coopera-
tive fully autonomous environment vulnerable to adversarial
attacks?
∙RQ-2 If the answer for RQ-1 turns out to be true, what
possible defense strategies can improve the CD-MARL policies’
robustness?
These are the research questions that this paper attempts to
address, given that the current literature has not explored these
questions, as extensively discussed in Section 2. The contributions
of the paper are the following:
∙We designed and trained two adversarial policies, namely
𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑐and 𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑠. The policies successfully identify adversarial
scenarios exposing the vulnerability of the victim policy (𝜋𝑣𝑐)
against adversarial attacks, used by CAVs. The adversaries
demonstrate a tangible impact, reducing the global reward of
𝜋𝑣𝑐to 26 and 47.5 using 𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑐and 𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑠, respectively.
∙The paper proposes adversarial training approaches to
improve the resilience of the 𝜋𝑣𝑐. The proposed method
showed its effectiveness by halving the collisions against 𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑐,
whereas, the safety risk is eliminated when facing 𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑠.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2
presents the existing works found in the literature. The details of
the victim policy and how adversarial attacks were executed are
explained in Section 3. Section 4 describes the defense approaches
used to mitigate damage caused by adversarial attacks. Finally,
Section 5 concludes the paper.

assumed the attacker is limited to black-box attacks, which are

episode, with the claim that a uniform approach is easy to detect
as done in [31, 32]. Lin et al. have also proposed a technique
called enchanting attack, where the adversary aims to force the
victim agent to arrive at a specific target state. This was done by
exploiting video generative models and a planning algorithm that
defines the perturbation inserted by the adversary to impose a
particular state on the victim agent. The suggested attacks were
applied on A3C and DQN. Gleave et al. [34] proposed a technique
to devise an adversarial policy that attacks the victim policy using
environment manipulation settings, where an adversary agent
acts as an opponent competing with the victim policy in zero-
sum game. The adversarial policy will be trained to maximize the
adversary’s opponent reward pushing to defeat the victim. Their
solution was evaluated in two-player game in MuJoCo games,
where the trained adversarial policy succeeded in outperforming
the victim agent.
2.2
AV Applications
This section continues to cover the literature with a closer look
at A-DRL uses in AV applications. Behzadan et al. [35] proposed
a general framework to leverage A-DRL to test the performance
of collision avoidance models deployed by AVs under worst-case
scenarios. The adversary agent is trained with various objectives,
namely, (1) causing direct collision with AV, (2) imposing the
AV to collide with another vehicle, and (3) interfering with the
desired path trajectory for the AV. They suggested evaluating
the performance of the adversarial policy based on episodes to
convergence, adversary reward, time to collision, and the amount
of damage caused. Wachi [36] work has utilized A-DRL to find
failure scenarios on rule-based AVs. This was achieved by having
multiple adversary agents interact with a rule-based model to
cause an accident or delay the arrival of the AV to the desired
destination. It was argued that a multi-adversary attack approach
will lead to a broad range of scenarios that can be used to improve
the state of the rule-based algorithm. He et al. [37] developed
robust LCD models to counteract the noise from inaccurate
measurement readings. These observational perturbations were
generated through black-box adversarial attacks using a Bayesian
optimization approach. A constrained observation-robust A2C
algorithm was subsequently designed and trained to operate in
AVs. In their later work, the same team [37] maintained the

Sharif et al. [38] have demonstrated the vulnerability of MARL-

an adversary. The considered environment in the study was

an adversarial AV. Two adversaries were trained with distinct

primary motivation of this work is to fill this research gap.

3.2
Attack Vector
In this subsection, details regarding how the adversarial attacks
are executed and adversary capabilities are presented. The attack
approach used in this work is Environment Manipulation. An
adversary CAV (𝐶𝐴𝑉𝑎) is introduced into an on-ramp merge sce-
nario populated by other CAVs operating under 𝜋𝑣𝑐. Importantly,
regular CAVs cannot distinguish between adversaries and regular
CAVs. The adversary will make black-box attacks, considering the
attacker has zero knowledge of the deployed model and model
weights. The capabilities of the 𝐶𝐴𝑉𝑎are limited to executing
actions in an environment shared with CAVs, without any direct
access to the sensory and actuator systems of other CAVs. Using
A-DRL, the adversary is trained based on two objectives, that aim
to disrupt the regular behavior of the deployed victim policy.

3.3.3
Reward Formula
The adversary agent is guided by a reward formula to produce
adversarial behavior, impacting the regular functioning of CAVs
operated by the victim policy. This work has opted to design two
distinct types of adversaries, each with a specific adversarial goal.
The details of each adversary are explained below:
∙Safety-Attacker: The adversary is trained to create adver-
sarial scenarios that maximize the chances of collision that

4
Defend Cooperative Policy
This section discusses the defense mechanism used to mitigate
the damage caused by 𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑐and 𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑠. In Section 3, we have
demonstrated how the adversarial attacks are performed against
𝜋𝑣𝑐where the results indicated it is vulnerable to both adversaries.
To address this vulnerability, an adversarial training technique
is utilized.
4.1
Adversarial Training
The concept of adversarial training aims to expose the DRL model
to adversarial samples with the intention that this can improve
the robustness of the deployed policy. Therefore, following train-
ing a policy with the absence of adversarial behavior, the obtained

4.2
Approaches
This work explored different procedures to obtain 𝜋that are
presented below.
4.2.1
Homogeneous Adversarial Training
The retrained policy is exposed to a single attacker out of
the ones discussed in Section 3.3.3. The objective is to have
𝜋minimize damage caused by a specific type of adversarial
behavior. 𝜋ℎ𝑚𝑐and 𝜋ℎ𝑚𝑠refer to retrained policies against 𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑐
and 𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑠, respectively.
4.2.2
Heterogeneous Adversarial Training
In an attempt to make the 𝜋generalized across adversaries, a
policy is retrained against both 𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑐and 𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑠. At every training
episode, the attacking adversary is selected randomly, with each
having an equal weight. The result of this is denoted as 𝜋ℎ𝑡.
4.2.3
Curriculum Learning Adversarial Training
The retraining is performed in two phases, where in each phase
the policy being trained gets attacked by a specific adversary
type. This also targets producing a generic policy against the
set of adversaries introduced in this work. Two policies can be
adversarially trained using this approach based on the order of
exposure. 𝜋𝑐𝑙𝑐is the policy that gets attacked by 𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑐during
the first phase, whereas 𝜋𝑐𝑙𝑠will interact with 𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑠during the
same phase.

5
Conclusion
In this research, the security of CD-MARL policies was explored
in terms of robustness against black-box adversarial attacks. By
applying A-DRL, we trained two adversarial policies, named 𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑐
and 𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑠, to produce adversarial behavior against the victim CD-
MARL policy, which is the policy operating the CAVs. These
policies successfully highlighted the vulnerabilities of the victim
policy, increasing accident likelihood and reducing global average
speed. Our findings indicate that the current state of CD-MARL
policies is not robust against adversarial attacks. To address
this issue, we implemented variations of adversarial training to
enhance the robustness of these policies. The policy exposed to
𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑐significantly reduced safety risks by half, while interaction
with 𝑎𝑑𝑣𝑠eliminated these risks. However, a limitation of our
study is the failure to develop policies robust against unseen types
of adversaries. Despite this, the results demonstrate the potential
of adversarial training as a defensive mechanism against attacks
on CD-MARL policies, highlighting an important direction for
future research in CAV security.

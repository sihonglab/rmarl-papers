1. Introduction

1.1. Background and Related Work
31
Due to their climatic benefits, low-carbon energy technologies, such as
32
wind and photovoltaics (PVs), have become a viable path to address sustain-
33
ability concerns in power systems [? ]. Particularly, there is now a growing
34
penetration of roof top PVs in Power Distribution Networks (PDNs) [? ?
35
]. However, widespread adoption of PVs has resulted in increased variability
36
and intermittency in power generation, thus increasing voltage fluctuations
37
in power systems [? ]. In addition to the fact that PDNs exhibit a high
38
resistance/inductance ratio, voltage fluctuations can pose significant chal-
39
lenges to the reliability and stability of power systems. Therefore, ensuring
40
the secure operation of modern PDNs requires sophisticated Active Voltage
41
Control (AVC) strategies that can dynamically respond to fast variations
42
in PV generation and load levels by leveraging controllable network devices
43
such as PV inverters and static var compensators [? ? ? ].
44
Traditionally, AVC strategies for PDNs are formulated as constrained
45
optimization problems and can be broadly categorized into two groups: op-
46
timal power flow (OPF)-based approaches [? ? ? ] and droop control-based
47
approaches [? ? ]. OPF-based methods address AVC by solving an optimiza-
48
tion problem that yields an optimal set of operating points for a given power
49
network while satisfying power balance constraints. For instance, Vovos et al.
50
[? ] formulate an OPF that minimizes power losses while enforcing both re-
51
active power and bus voltage limits. When the system model and parameters
52
are fully known, OPF-based methods can deliver near-optimal AVC perfor-
53
mance; however, acquiring and continuously updating such detailed system
54
information is often expensive and challenging, which constitutes a key lim-
55
itation of this class of methods [? ]. Researchers are also exploring the use
56
of neural networks to approximate OPF solutions, offering rapid predictions
57
of optimal setpoints for static system snapshots but relies on high-quality
58
OPF labels and may degrade when operating conditions change. Reinforce-
59
ment learning (RL), on the other hand, learns a control policy directly from
60
interaction with the grid, allowing it to cope with dynamics, uncertainty,
61

62

63
ing tap changes into its reward (feedback signal). The trade-off is that RL
64
does not inherently ensure strict feasibility unless safety layers are added. In
65
contrast to OPF-based methods, droop control-based methods aim to define
66
a piecewise linear relationship between PV generation and voltage, which
67
is then used to regulate local bus voltages without requiring global voltage

70
reducing their adaptability across diverse systems. Consequently, as PDNs
71
grow in scale and complexity as a result of the integration of renewable en-
72

74

Due to the limitations of traditional AVC techniques and the success
76
of single-agent RL in various control problems [? ? ], researchers in the
77
power systems community have started applying DRL to AVC problem [?
78
? ]. Unlike classical methods that depend on predefined models and ex-
79
tensive manual tuning, DRL-based approaches learn control policies directly
80
from their interactions with the system. This allows them to naturally cope
81
with nonlinear dynamics, uncertainty, and time-varying operating conditions
82
without the need for explicit modeling. Moreover, once trained, DRL con-
83
trollers are capable of making fast real-time decisions and can more readily
84
adapt to changes in grid conditions caused by renewable output variability
85
or load fluctuations than conventional techniques. However, as the network
86
grows in size and complexity, the effectiveness and scalability of single-agent
87
DRL approaches deteriorate because a single controller must process high-
88
dimensional system states and simultaneously manage multiple controllable
89
devices. In addition, by being centralized, these DRL-based AVC architec-
90
tures are vulnerable to a single point of failure.
91

92
to meet the demand for fast response and effective coordination, deep MARL,
93
a distributed and data-driven framework for multi-agent control problems [?
94

96

97
]. In region-based schemes, the PDN is partitioned into several regions ac-

1.2. Contributions
160
This paper presents an in-depth investigation of topology-aware MARL
161
policies for distributed AVC in the centralized training and decentralized exe-
162

163

164

165

166

167
these studies typically evaluate performance only on small- to medium-scale
168
systems (e.g., IEEE-33 and IEEE-141). Furthermore, the proposed GNN
169
policies in these works are typically benchmarked against standard vector-
170
based MARL baselines that use considerably simpler architectures with far
171
fewer parameters. To fairly examine the performance advantages and ro-
172
bustness properties of GNN-based policies under conditions comparable to
173
the standard topology-unaware MARL baselines, we use our GNN-MARL

5. Discussion
679
This section presents the design rationale behind the performance and
680
topology robustness of GNN policies. It also provides guidance on selecting
681
appropriate types of GNN policy networks to achieve optimal GNN-MARL
682
performance across different systems, taking into account network size, com-
683
putational constraints, and robustness to topology changes. Furthermore, it
684
addresses key considerations in choosing suitable voltage barrier functions.
685
5.1. Design Rationale for Topology Robustness of GNN Policies
686
The results of our experiments show that GNN policies maintains high
687
controllability on both radial and looped power networks (Tables 2 – 5),
688
with consistent VOC, CR, and PL improvements over conventional MARL
689
approaches with RNN policies. Two design choices explain this robustness:
690
(i) permutation-equivariant graph encoders preserve relational structure of
691
power networks, enabling transfer across network topologies, and (ii) edge
692
conditioning (switch states, impedances) exposes energized network paths to
693
the GNN-MARL policies. Further, the optimal shallow-to-moderate GNN
694
depth aligns with localized electrical influence in distribution networks, thus
695
explaining why GNN-MARL with GCN/GAT policy networks excel as net-
696
work size increases while avoiding the over-smoothing effects that affect many
697
GNN models.
698
5.2. On when to Choose Different GNN Policy Network Architectures
699
Our detailed evaluations of GNN-MARL across several test networks re-
700
veal distinct performance patterns that can guide architecture selection for
701
28
This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=5960743
Preprint not peer reviewed

practical deployments. From the evaluations results, we observe that GNN
702
architecture choice for MARL policy networks in voltage control application
703
depends on network characteristics, computational constrains, and perfor-
704
mance priorities.
705
5.2.1. Network Size Considerations
706
From 4, we observe that for smaller networks (IEEE-33), the GNN-MARL
707
approach with the GCN-BL policy delivers the best performance (slightly
708
surpassing GAT-BL) and also achieves the best VOC and PL performance.
709
However, as the size of the network increases to IEEE-141, the GGCN model
710
combined with the Bowl barrier function becomes the most effective, yielding
711
the best overall results. For the largest network considered (IEEE-322), GAT-
712
BL performs best (with results similar to GAT-L1), providing the strongest
713
voltage control performance among all GNN-MARL configurations.
714
We also observe that for all test systems, the GAT-based policy is either
715
the best or the second best performing policy, thus making it the overall
716
best GNN policy. This can be explained by the fact that, unlike GCN and
717
GGCN, GAT learns how important each neighbor is instead of weighting all
718
neighbors equally or relaying on fixed gates, thus making GAT more flexible
719
on graphs where neighbors contribute unevenly and more robust to noisy or
720
irrelevant edges (which is the case with the power networks).
721
5.2.2. Computational Efficiency Trade-offs
722
Among the GNN-based policies examined in this study, GCN provides the
723
most computationally efficient option with a complexity of O(|E|), making
724
it well-suited for real-time deployment and small-scale networks, where it
725
delivers strong performance with excellent power efficiency. GAT, in contrast,
726
increases representational power through attention mechanisms but incurs a
727
complexity of O(|E|·H), where H is the number of attention heads; it proves
728
particularly advantageous for large-scale power networks while still achieving
729
low power losses. The GNN-MARL approach with a GGCN policy attains the
730
best results on medium-scale networks with competitive power efficiency, but
731
its performance degrades on larger networks. This is partly due to the fact
732
that GGCN relies on recurrent gated message passing with a computational
733
complexity of O(T ·|E|·D2) and space complexity of O(T ·|V |·D), where T is
734
the number of unrolling steps (typically 3–5) and D is the hidden dimension.
735

5.2.3. Topology Robustness
736
From our experiments, we find that all examined GNN architectures de-
737
liver stable performance on both radial and looped network topologies, keep-
738
ing CR within a 5% variance and achieving competitive power losses (in the
739
0.055–0.077 MW range for the looped case). This robustness arises from the
740
permutation-equivariant property of GNN encoders, which preserves struc-
741
tural relationships in the network even as the topology changes. In contrast,
742
the RNN baselines exhibit a substantial drop in CR performance (20–30%)
743
when transitioning between different topologies.
744
5.2.4. Edge Feature Utilization
745
From our experiments, we find that explicitly modeling edge features pro-
746
vides a modest advantage in small networks, where switch statuses and line
747
impedances play a major role in local voltage regulation. However, for larger
748
networks, implicit edge information modeling through adjacency matrices
749
proves sufficient. This suggests that direct edge feature encoding becomes
750
less important as network connectivity grows. This finding has direct impli-
751
cations in real-world deployment, as it removes the need to collect detailed
752
transmission line characteristics, which is difficult and an extra burden to
753
obtain in most cases, for achieving better GNN-MARL performance on the
754
voltage control problem.
755
5.3. Barrier Function Selection
756
The choice of voltage barrier function significantly influences the learn-
757
ing and generalization ability of MARL approaches in voltage control tasks.
758
Consequently, we tested several barrier functions in our experiments. We
759
find that the impact of the chosen barrier on the performance of both GNN-
760
MARL and the baseline MARL with an RNN policy depends on the specific
761
characteristics of the power network and the policy architecture.
For in-
762
stance, on the IEEE-33 test system, GNN-MARL with GCN-BL and GAT-
763
BL policies achieved the best results, whereas GGCN with a BL barrier
764
yielded inferior performance compared to GGCN policies using L1 or L2
765
barrier functions. This pattern is consistently observed across all networks
766
considered in our evaluation. Therefore, determining a universally effective
767
voltage barrier function, one that remains optimal regardless of the choice of
768
the policy network, the size of the PDN or the characteristics of the system,
769
remains an open challenge.

6. Conclusion and Future Directions
771
GNN-based multi-agent reinforcement learning (GNN-MARL) consistently
772
outperforms conventional MARL with topology unaware policies for voltage
773
control across all evaluated distribution networks, with the performance mar-
774
gin widening as both network size and structural complexity increase. The
775
graph convolutional network with Bowl barrier function (GCN-BL) performs
776
best on small networks (IEEE-33), gated graph convolutional networks with
777
Bowl barrier function (GGCN-BL) on medium networks (IEEE-141), and
778
graph attention newtorks with Bowl barrier function (GAT-BL) on large net-
779
works (IEEE-322). Across these settings, GNN-MARL achieves low voltage
780
constraint violations (VOC), high constraint satisfaction rates (CR), and im-
781
proved power loss (PL) efficiency. Among the GNN architectures examined
782
in this study, the GAT-based policy achieved the strongest overall perfor-
783
mance, ranking either first or second in every scenario considered. This can
784
be attributed to the fact that, in contrast to GCN and GGCN, GAT ex-
785
plicitly learns the relevance of each neighbor rather than assigning uniform
786
weights or relying on predetermined gates. As a result, GAT is better suited
787
to graphs where neighbors have uneven influence and is more resilient to
788
noisy or irrelevant edges, which is generally the case in power networks.
789
To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first MARL-based study
790
to demonstrate successful voltage regulation in looped (meshed) distribu-
791
tion systems, achieving nearly the same performance as in radial networks
792
while preserving low power losses. Compared to the traditional optimization-
793
based OPF benchmark, GNN-MARL closely tracks VOC and CR on small
794
networks, exhibits more pronounced performance gaps on medium-sized sys-
795
tems, and exceeds OPF in terms of VOC on the largest network, while con-
796
sistently delivering lower power losses than both OPF and topology-unaware
797
MARL.
798
Further, our experiments show that high-level topology information (i.e.,
799
the adjacency matrix) is sufficient for learning effective control policies: the
800
inclusion of explicit edge attributes (such as line impedances) yields nearly
801
identical performance. Collectively, these findings highlight GNN-MARL as
802
a scalable, topology-aware, and energy-efficient framework for distributed
803
voltage control in modern power grids with high renewable penetration, re-
804
silient to different and looped network configurations, and less sensitive to
805
topology-induced retraining requirements.
806
Future research should explore spatio-temporal GNN models for evolving

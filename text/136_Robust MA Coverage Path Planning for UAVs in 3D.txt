Robust Multi-Agent Coverage Path Planning for Unmanned Airial
Vehicles (UAVs) in Complex 3D Environments with Deep Reinforcement
Learning*
Julian Bialas1, Mario Doeller2 and Robert Kathrein3
Abstract— Multi-agent coverage path planning (MACPP)
with unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) is an essential task in
several real-world applications, such as environmental moni-
toring, surveillance, and search and rescue missions. Existing
MACPP algorithms suffer from limitations, such as the inability
to generalize over all system parameters, long computational
time, and limited applicability in dynamic environments. In
this study, we propose a novel MACPP framework based on
proximal policy optimization (PPO), a model-free reinforcement
learning algorithm. Our framework provides a dynamic 3D
machine learning-based approach that can generalize over
all system parameters, such as target zones, agent positions,
battery levels, and other critical factors. Hence, the framework
offers several advantages, including efficient and effective path
planning, real-time application on hardware, and applicability
in complex 3D structures such as buildings or alpine terrain. We
demonstrate the feasibility and effectiveness of our approach
by successfully applying it in real-time on hardware. Our
experimental results show that our proposed framework can
effectively handle 3D structures and react to changes mid-flight.
I. INTRODUCTION
Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) have become an in-
creasingly popular tool for a wide range of applications,
including environmental monitoring, search and rescue oper-
ations, and military surveillance. In many of these applica-
tions, coverage path planning (CPP) is an essential task that
must be performed by the UAVs [1]. CPP involves finding a
path that covers a given area with minimal overlap, ensuring
that the coverage of the target area (TA) is maximized within
the constraints. Multi-agent CPP (MACPP) is a variant of
this problem where multiple UAVs must work together to
cover a larger area more efficiently. The MACPP problem
is challenging because it requires solving the NP-hard CPP
problem while ensuring coordination with other agents [2].
In order to react to unpredictable changes in the mission,
such as an unforeseen termination or collisions of an agent
or changes in the TA, the replanning of the trajectory needs
to be feasible in real-time.

Researchers have proposed several genetic algorithm-
based MACPP methods that perform well in complex 3D
environments. For instance, the authors of [3] proposed a
sampling-based algorithm that uses a grid-based representa-
tion of the environment and uses an encoding and decoding
strategy for the state processing. Similarly, Li et al. proposed
a genetic algorithm-based method that samples viewpoints to
optimize coverage and path lengths for multiple UAVs [4].
While these methods perform well in offline environments,
they have a long calculation time and cannot adapt to
dynamic environments.
Linear
optimization-based
methods
have
also
been
proposed for MACPP. Melo et al. proposed a linear
optimization-based method that is combined with a heuristic
to find the optimal paths for multiple UAVs [5]. This method
works well in 3D environments and can handle dynamic
environments by reoptimzation. However, it also has a high
computational time, and the model may not be scalable for
large-scale problems. To overcome the limitation of static
approaches, machine learning-based approaches seems to
be a promising alternative. The authors of [6] proposed a
MACPP algorithm based on Multi-Agent Deep Deterministic
Policy Gradient (MADDPG) algorithm to solve the CPP
problem. The MADDPG algorithm learns a centralized critic
and decentralized actor networks for each UAV, which can
generalize well over map parameters. However, this method
is only applicable to 2D maps and may not be applicable in
3D environments as the scanning of vertical walls is not
guaranteed (e.g for inspection). The same applies to the
framework proposed by Bayerlein et al. [7], which is based
on deep reinforcement learning. Another machine learning-
based MACPP algorithm is proposed in [8]. This method
only assigns separated target areas to the respective agents
with no dynamic flight pattern within the region.
Our framework is based on our previous work as described
in [9], where we demonstrated the deep reinforcement model
on a single-agent system. This paper extends the previous
work with the following research contributions:
• Extend the single agent system to a dynamic decentral-
ized executed multi-agent CPP system.
• Introduce a communication procedure to share outdoor
coverage information.
• Indoor experiments to simulate the ability of the system
to react to unpredictable events.

II. METHODS
A. Problem Formulation
This study employs a three-dimensional grid map, denoted
as m ∈M, where M = Bw×d×h and w, d, h ∈N represent
the map’s width, depth, and height. Each element in the
boolean matrix m signifies the presence or absence of an
element of the point cloud in the corresponding cube in the
environment. The function y : T →M 6 represents the target
cells at time-step t ∈T, where T = {t0, t1, . . . , tterminal}
denotes the set of possible time-steps. The value range M 6
corresponds to each target cube in the map, possessing six
faces that can be covered individually. The grid map is
represented by a three-dimensional matrix of size M. The
presence of n ∈N \ {0, 1} agents is assumed in the map. At
each time-step t ∈T, the agents cover the area specified
by v : T →M 6, with a precise definition provided in
reference [9]. The TA that is yet to be covered is obtained
using y(t+1) = y(t)∧¬v(t), as described in reference [10].
The aim of the study is to maximize the coverage of the TA
while beginning and ending in a designated start-and-landing
zone (SLZ), avoiding no-flying zones (NFZ), and taking the
battery level into account.

C. Environment and Motion Model
We utilize the environment and motion model proposed
in [9] for our multi-agent framework. The agent is assumed to
be equipped with an observation sensor on each side and we
impose orientation-aware coverage such that each sensor can
only cover the area that is opposite to its viewing direction.
For instance, the downward-facing sensor can only cover
cells that are facing upwards. Moreover, the coverage area
of a single observation sensor is assumed to be a pyramid
with a floor space of 5 × 5 cells and a height of three. If a
target cell’s view is blocked, it is not considered as covered.
The motion model is adapted from [10] and extended by
incorporating the up and down actions. If the agent takes
an action that leads to a collision with the environment
or another agent or enters into a no-fly zone (NFZ), the
agent will hover instead. This motion model is based on the
assumption of a safety controller on top of the reinforcement
learning model. Additionally, we have developed a safe land-
ing algorithm to ensure optimal utilization of the movement
budget as defined in ??.
Each agent has a constraint on its power consumption,
similar to [10]. A movement budget b : T × U →N is given
to each agent, representing its power level. The movement
budget is decremented with each time-step.

3) Reward Function:
The reward function R(s, s′) is
defined by four components. Firstly, a positive constant is
given for each newly covered cell. Secondly, a positive
constant is assigned if full coverage of the area is achieved
within the current time-step. Thirdly, a negative constant is
given if the agent collides with the environment or other
agents. Lastly, a negative reward is assigned if the agent tries
to navigate into an NFZ. This reward function is designed
to encourage the agent to explore new areas, achieve full
coverage efficiently, avoid collisions, and adhere to flying
regulations.
D. Training Process
In order to enhance the generalization of the model,
it is trained on a set of randomly generated map repre-
sentations. These maps are created by randomly sampling
geometric shapes to generate the respective zones. The three-
dimensional target zone is obtained by reversing the equation
defined in (8). The height information used in the model is
derived from data collected by the Austrian government as
part of the Airborne Laser Scan project1. From a collection
of point clouds, one is randomly selected and then restricted
to a region of interest. The desired map size is determined
by applying average pooling. Additionally, the map size can
be further reduced by increasing the pooling filter size and
stride. The movement budget for each episode is determined
by drawing a sample from a predefined interval with a
uniform distribution. Once the model has been trained for a
specified number of episodes on a randomly generated map,
a new one is generated. An overview of the final parameters
is provided in Table I.

In the case of larger agent groups, the transmission process
should exclusively target neighboring agents, a subject that
will be addressed in forthcoming research endeavors.

A. Simulation Results
Just like [9] we define the coverage ratio as evaluation
criteria. In this study, we conducted a Monte Carlo simulation
[12] to evaluate and compare the performance of a single-
agent system versus a multi-agent system for coverage path
planning on randomly generated maps. Figure 2 presents
the results of the simulation. They demonstrate that the
multi-agent system’s coverage efficiency increases rapidly
compared to the single-agent system as the agent is granted

more movement budget. This is evident from the steeper
slope of the multi-agent system’s coverage ratio compared
to the single-agent system, which indicates its superiority in
covering more area within a given time frame. Furthermore,
the smaller interquartile range of the multi-agent system
suggests a lower variance and higher robustness, suggesting
the system’s ability to perform consistently under varying
conditions. Additionally, hardly any outliers in the multi-
agent system are observed. As the TA, the SLZ, the move-
ment budget and 3D structure of the map are generated
randomly, the results of the Monte Carlo simulation also
demonstrate that the model can generalize over all these
parameters. Additionally this proves, as the prediction of at
in the state st is independent of st−1, that the model can
handle in-flight changes of agents positions or changes in
the TA.
B. Experimental Results
Another objective of this study was to demonstrate the fea-
sibility of running a dynamic system algorithm on hardware
and to highlight the advantages of a dynamic system over
a static one. To achieve this goal, we conducted a series of
experiments using the Crazyflie 2.1 quadcopters [13] and the
CrazySwarm framework [14]. The CrazySwarm framework
is a system architecture that allows the control of an indoor
swarm of miniature quadcopters with the assistance of a
motion capture system. This system provides the ability to
send control commands to selected agents and receive state
information from them. Due to the limited computational
resources of the micro UAVs, we ran the processes of the
agents on a centralized computer with a relatively weak
processor (i5 5th generation). The control commands and
agent states were then streamed between the control station
and the agents.
In order to demonstrate the effectiveness of the system, we
conducted an experiment with three agents in a generated
environment. The results of the experiment are shown in
Figure 3. The laboratory environment depicted in Figure
3c displays the Crazyflie 2.1 quadcopters, positioned at
their designated start and landing zones. The experimental
procedure involved testing the agents’ capabilities based on
the RL model, with the aim of covering over 99% of the
target area while successfully returning to the respective start
and landing zones within the movement constraints. The
results of the first test run indicated a success in achieving the
desired outcomes. The model simulation of the first test run
is illustrated in Figure 3a, while the corresponding actual tra-
jectories are displayed in Figure 3b. In a subsequent test run,
a deliberate attempt was made to disrupt the functioning of an
agent by intentionally causing the third agent (left trajectory
in Figure 3d and 3e) to collide. Notably, a dynamic change
in the trajectory of the remaining agents was observed.
Therefore, the second agent managed to cover the area that
was initially assigned to the third agent. This experiment
demonstrated the adaptability of the model to environmental
changes, as well as the successful transferability of the model
to hardware.

IV. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORKS
A. Conclusions
In conclusion, our proposed algorithm for multi-agent
coverage path planning with UAVs based on deep rein-
forcement learning shows promising results in handling
the challenges associated with dynamic and complex 3D
environments. The structural configuration of the framework
facilitates its applicability across diverse scenarios, such as
building inspections or search and rescue missions, wherein
the operator necessitates real-time adjustment of the TA mid-
flight. The hardware testing validates its suitability for real-
world applications.
B. Future Works
In the subsequent phase of this research, the proposed
framework for multi-agent coverage path planning with un-
manned aerial vehicles will be further evaluated in outdoor
scenarios with autonomous UAVs equipped with companion
computers. In order to enable complete autonomy of the
agents, a communication protocol needs to be developed to
allow the exchange of coverage information among them. To
achieve this, we plan to utilize the emerging 5G and LoraWan
communication technologies, which provide high-speed and
long-range communication capabilities.
Moreover, to further improve the efficiency and effective-
ness of the proposed model, additional models that evaluate
sensor information will be incorporated into the system. This
integration of sensor models will enable the agents to detect
changing environmental conditions and optimize coverage
efficiency based on real-time sensor data. Currently, this
research is being conducted in the Josef Ressel Center for
multimedia analysis in the mobility domain, where various
sensor models are being evaluated.
